# Positive pattern: derive a marked-GW flow rule before using its exact-dimension interface

**Evidence:** Rousselin, *Invariant Measures, Hausdorff Dimension and Dimension Drop of some Harmonic Measures on Galton–Watson Trees*, arXiv:1708.06965v2, the canonical 37-page paper accepted for the restricted prior use. See the canonical source card（本机附件：`evidence/source_cards/ROUSSELIN2017HARMONIC.md`）. This is the paper containing Theorems 2.2 and 3.2 discussed here.

Work on its marked Galton–Watson model: `p₀=0`, `p₁<1`, finite mean offspring `m`, iid marks independent of offspring and descendant subtrees, and a positive measurable flow rule consistent under subtree reindexing. To invoke Theorem 3.2, match the source’s recursive observation `φ(T)=h(Γ_root, Σ_i φ(T[i]))` and the specific source-defined flow built from that observation; an arbitrary positive subtree-consistent flow plus the three identities is not enough to conclude invariance of the `κ(φ(T))` density. Then verify the symmetry, associativity, and position-of-summand identities for `h`, and check `0 < C=E_GW[κ(φ(T))] < ∞`. Theorem 2.2 then applies when a `Θ`-stationary probability is absolutely continuous with respect to GW: it is equivalent to GW, the stationary system is ergodic and unique within that class, and the flow is exact-dimensional in the natural common-prefix ultrametric, with dimension `E_{μΘ}[-log Θ_T(Ξ₁)]`.

Do not transfer this theorem to a deterministic pair of Cantor codings. The 22-page arXiv:1711.07920v1 Rousselin paper is a distinct L13 full read with zero accepted G25-use credit; no theorem or local proof caveat from it is attached to this example. The local issue in Theorem 5.2 of the accepted 37-page paper concerns a different, later dimension-drop argument and is not used in this Theorems 2.2/3.2 pattern.

A correct transfer checklist is:

1. Verify the GW offspring and iid-mark model, including the no-leaves and finite-mean assumptions.
2. Check flow positivity, measurability and subtree consistency; for Theorem 3.2 verify the exact recursion `φ(T)=h(Γ_root, Σ_i φ(T[i]))`, the source-defined induced flow, all three identities for `h`, and finite positive normalization `C`. Do not substitute an arbitrary consistent flow for the flow constructed from `φ`.
3. Verify the required `Θ`-stationary probability is absolutely continuous with respect to GW before invoking Theorem 2.2.
4. Use the natural common-prefix metric and the exact-dimensional conclusion only in this model; prove any additional coding or target-space bridge separately.

## Positive pattern: match the coding entropy theorem to its model

For a symbolic invariant probability `m` and IFS code `π`, Feng–Hu's projection entropy records the entropy visible after coding. If the law is ergodic and the IFS is `m`-conformal, Theorem 2.8 gives `dim(m∘π⁻¹)=h_π/∫λ dm`; for a product IFS, Theorem 2.11 uses a sum of coordinate projection-entropy increments over the ordered Lyapunov exponents. Before using either, check the exact shift law, code, overlap treatment, conformality, positive finite exponent, and ball/cylinder bridge. A nonergodic law has a conditional component formula rather than one constant rate. See the Feng–Hu card（本机附件：`evidence/source_cards/EXSRC-5f77fbd5f70438b9-FENG-HU-PROJECTION-ENTROPY.md`）.

Hochman–Shmerkin give a different route when rescalings form an ergodic CP-chain: average the projected entropy at finite scale, carry its `O(1/q)` loss, and use their tree-morphism transfer to obtain an image lower-dimension bound. Their product-projection theorem for multiplicatively independent `T_m,T_n` laws and their regular-I.F.S./Gibbs theorem have separate invariance, separation, support, and minimality assumptions. Neither route proves equivalence or singularity of two fixed measures on one target. See the Hochman–Shmerkin card（本机附件：`evidence/source_cards/EXSRC-ffe34e1b8d1959fa-HOCHMAN-SHMERKIN-LOCAL-ENTROPY.md`） and the joint coding-entropy use card（本机附件：`evidence/usage_cards/G25-coding-entropy-dimension.md`）.

## Positive pattern: conditional common hyperbolic boundary

Suppose two finitely supported symmetric random walks generate the same non-elementary hyperbolic group `Γ`, both hit the same boundary `∂Γ`, and the two target measures are pushed through the same Borel map `φ:∂Γ→E`. BHM Corollary 1.6 lets one prove their boundary measures equivalent by verifying the global two-sided Green-function comparison (or either stated cross-entropy/Green-speed limit); equivalence then passes through `φ`. Alternatively, for the finitely supported admissible setting of Tanaka, if each walk satisfies `h_i=l_i v` for the same growth rate `v` and visual metric, each is comparable to the same boundary Hausdorff measure and their pushforwards are equivalent. These are model-specific equivalence routes; they do not imply singularity when their tests fail. The common `Γ` and common `φ` are substantive hypotheses absent from A147 as currently stated. See the comparison use card（本机附件：`evidence/usage_cards/G25-hyperbolic-boundary-comparison.md`）.


## Positive pattern: compare exact dimensions on one common Floyd boundary

Let two admissible finite-first-moment random walks be given on one relatively hyperbolic group G. Fix the same Floyd parameter λ and hence the same Floyd boundary metric. On the Floyd boundary, apply Dussaule–Yang Theorem 1.1 or the safely closed part of Theorem 3.1 only for λ≥λ₀ on the Floyd or Bowditch boundary; the printed all-λ Floyd clause is excluded from positive use by R13 because the paper’s standing range and final proof use λ≥λ₀. If both harmonic measures satisfy the corresponding exact-dimensional theorem and their h_i/l_i ratios differ, their exact dimensions differ. The full-measure sets on which those local dimensions hold then have different Hausdorff dimensions, so they form a common-space separator and the measures are mutually singular. This final set argument is derived from exact dimensionality; it is not stated as a pairwise singularity theorem by Dussaule–Yang.

Le Prince provides a different pattern: choose the symmetric finite-support generating law constructed in Theorem 3.4, then Corollary 3.5 gives a harmonic measure singular to Hausdorff measure for the chosen visual metric. This proves existence of such a walk, not singularity for a previously specified walk. The singularity conclusions are on the hyperbolic boundary; to transfer them to E, a common map must preserve the relevant measure classes (for example, be a Borel isomorphism on full-measure sets) or a Borel separator must be proved directly in E. A common measurable map alone is insufficient. Neither pattern currently reduces A147. See Dussaule–Yang（本机附件：`evidence/source_cards/EXSRC-c9838fae60c2262f-DUSSAULE-YANG-RELATIVE-HYPERBOLIC-DIMENSION.md`）, Le Prince（本机附件：`evidence/source_cards/EXSRC-b05233923e45b9d0-LE-PRINCE-HYPERBOLIC-SINGULARITY.md`）, and the conditional use card（本机附件：`evidence/usage_cards/G25-hyperbolic-boundary-comparison.md`）.


## Positive pattern: use a joint spectrum only for its specified tree and law pair

For one nondegenerate Galton–Watson tree satisfying the all-moment assumptions in Kinnison–Mörters (2010), compare the generation-uniform branching measure with the paper’s forward-only child-uniform boundary law. Theorem 2.1 supplies the cross-constraint on their local dimensions, and the nontrivial Theorem 2.2 spectra additionally require the Schröder condition `P(N=1)>0`; nondegeneracy gives `0<P(N=1)<1`, making `τ=−log P(N=1)/log m` finite. First verify the offspring law, the natural common-prefix metric, and that the second law really chooses a child uniformly at every step. This result does not identify that forward law with undirected simple-random-walk harmonic measure, and its spectrum does not itself prove equivalence or singularity. See the joint branching-measure use card（本机附件：`evidence/usage_cards/G25-branching-measures.md`） and source card（本机附件：`evidence/source_cards/EXSRC-13fbec7a861f433d-KINNISON-MORTERS2010-JOINT-MULTIFRACTAL.md`）; R13 v8 accepted this scoped G25.04 use.


## Positive pattern: separate two base-coded laws by a typical digit frequency

For the specific compact set `A={Σ ε_n 10^{-2n}: ε_n∈{0,…,90}}`, Benjamini–Peres Example 6 compares the base-10 and base-100 tree-walk harmonic measures on the same target. The base-100 digits are iid under each law, with symbol-90 frequency `1/91` for the base-100 walk and `>1/40` for the base-10 walk. Since the digits uniquely code points of `A`, the Borel set where the limiting frequency is greater than `1/40` is a common-target separator by the strong law. This is a worked base-change example, not a finite-type transducer theorem and not the binary/ternary middle-third Cantor problem. R13 accepted this restricted use for the example; it adds one G25 credit but does not establish the A147 conclusion.
