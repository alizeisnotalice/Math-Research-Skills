## Information and arrival model

The source studies independent nonnegative values with one sample from each distribution (except where explicitly stated otherwise). For its single-sample prophet results, the order adversary sees the realized values in advance before choosing the order; the online algorithm does not know the distributions beyond the samples (abstract and §1.1, PDF pp. 1–2). This is stronger than M0049’s online weight-adaptive adversary, which cannot see the current item’s value when choosing which item arrives next.

## Main prophet guarantees

- **Theorem 1 (PDF p. 5):** a black-box conversion turns an order-oblivious secretary algorithm with ratio `α` into a single-sample prophet algorithm with ratio `α`. The conversion needs the order-oblivious property; a secretary algorithm whose policy depends on the random permutation does not qualify automatically.
- **Theorem 2 (PDF pp. 6–7):** for a `k`-uniform matroid, the Rehearsal algorithm has ratio `1−O(1/√k)`, asymptotically optimal in k. It uses a single sample per item under the paper’s adversarial value-aware ordering. The proof pairs each sample with its realized value, couples them into a correlated walk, and bounds the top-k acceptance loss through a Chernoff analysis of the walk’s height; there is no exact constant asserted by the `O(·)` statement.
- **Theorem 3 (PDF pp. 7–9; detailed proof Appendix D, pp. 16–18):** in degree-d bipartite matching, `d²+1` samples yield competitive ratio `1/6.75=4/27`. For each edge, an edge-specific index chooses its pricing sample; the threshold is the minimum weight needed for that edge to belong to a maximum-weight matching when other weights are fixed. At online arrival, a coin keeps the edge with probability 1/3; a kept edge is accepted only if its value exceeds its price and it remains matching-feasible. The proof’s two incident-neighborhood collision bounds each cost at most 1/3, leaving joint survival at least 4/9; multiplying by 1/3 yields 4/27. The algorithm’s sample count and graph-degree assumption are essential (PDF p. 8; rendered).

## Mechanism applications and boundaries

Section 5 transfers comparison-based welfare algorithms to revenue in specified settings: for IID regular distributions, the reserve-price construction preserves the algorithm’s ratio up to the stated reserve argument; for nonidentical MHR distributions the paper gives its own `1/(2e)`-type revenue conversion. Theorem 6 gives a free-order matroid posted-price result: for independent MHR distributions, one sample suffices for revenue ratio `1/(8e)` and welfare ratio `1/8`; for independent regular distributions, the same algorithm gives revenue ratio `1/8` (PDF pp. 20–21). These are mechanism results with regularity/MHR and order-control assumptions, not consequences of the prophet theorem alone.

The matching algorithm in Theorem 3 is stated for degree-d bipartite graphs; arbitrary bipartite matching and general matroid intersections require different sample counts or arguments. Theorem 2 is asymptotic in k, not a claimed exact finite-k ratio. Keep sample knowledge, adversarial order, feasibility, and expected maximum feasible weight together as one theorem model.
