## Model and results

Every graph edge e has an independent weight drawn from an unknown distribution D_e. Before online decisions, the algorithm receives one independent sample s_e from each D_e; the realized online edge weight r_e is another draw.

- **Theorem 1 (pp. 5–8):** for maximum-weight matching in an arbitrary graph with adversarial edge arrivals, Algorithm 1 is 16-competitive in expectation, i.e. 16 E[w(M)]≥E[OPT]. The algorithm first computes a greedy matching on the sample graph. Each vertex is priced at the sample weight of its incident greedy edge (zero if unmatched). An arriving edge is price-feasible only if its realized weight exceeds both endpoint prices, and is accepted when both endpoints are free.
- **Theorem 2 (pp. 9–11):** for bipartite matching with adversarial one-sided vertex arrivals, Algorithm 3 is 8-competitive in expectation. All incident realized edge weights of a buyer arrive together. The buyer's candidate is its heaviest edge that beats both endpoint prices; it is accepted if its item endpoint remains free.
- **Theorem 3 (pp. 11–13):** there is a truthful, 16-approximate mechanism for bipartite matching with one-sided vertex arrivals. It offers each remaining item at the maximum of the buyer and item sample-greedy prices; the buyer selects an available feasible item maximizing reported value minus price. The 8-approximation's “choose the highest-weight feasible edge” rule is not truthful, so its guarantee is not asserted for this mechanism.

## Main proof mechanism

For Theorems 1–2, the paper couples the online procedure with an offline process that draws two independent values per edge, sorts them in descending order, and uses a fair coin to assign the first encountered copy to sample-side greedy or real-side price-feasible edges; the twin copy is assigned to the other side. Claim 1 (general graph) and Claim 2 (bipartite vertex arrival) establish that the relevant sets E′, M_S, and M have the same distributions as in the online algorithm.

Greedy on the sample is a 2-approximation to the maximum matching, and the coupling yields E[w(E′)]≥(1/2)E[w(M_S)]. For general edge arrivals, the proof defines the largest E′-incident edge e_v at each vertex and a “safe” condition requiring no smaller competing E′ edge at either endpoint. Each such edge is safe with probability at least 1/4. A charging argument handles conflicts and gives E[w(M)]≥(1/8)E[w(M_S)]≥(1/16)E[OPT]. For one-sided vertex arrivals, a batch-specific safe-edge argument improves the safety probability to at least 1/2 and yields 1/8 overall.

For Theorem 3, the buyer's utility-maximizing assignment is shown to be a maximal matching in the same E′ set. The same safe-edge analysis as in the general graph proof then gives 1/16; independence of buyer values from posted prices gives truthfulness.

## Scope and limits

These are **single-sample, unknown-distribution** guarantees with independent edge values. The first result is general-graph edge arrival; the second and third require bipartite one-sided vertex arrivals. The online order is adversarial; the paper notes its bounds also hold against an adaptive adversary that knows the realized sequence. Do not treat these sample-based approximation factors as full-information prophet ratios, as random-order results, or as ex-ante/OCRS statements.
