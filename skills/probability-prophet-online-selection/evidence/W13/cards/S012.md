## Main theorem and model scope

For n independent nonnegative random variables, the prophet-secretary model reveals them in a uniformly random order; the free-order model lets the gambler choose the order. In both sample-based variants, the distributions are unknown but the gambler receives k independent samples from each distribution before the online phase.

**Theorem 1.1 (p. 5):** for every ε>0, k≥O(ε⁻⁵) samples per distribution suffice for a randomized stopping policy with expected reward at least (C_S−ε)E[max_i X_i] in prophet secretary and at least (C_F−ε)E[max_i X_i] in free order, where C_S and C_F are the respective optimal full-information ratios. A qualifying policy can be computed in polynomial time. The theorem approaches the optimal constants; it does not identify their numerical values, and it does not say the guarantee is exactly C_S or C_F with a fixed finite sample count.

## Proof architecture and exact interfaces

1. **Upper-tail insensitivity (Propositions 1.2–1.5, pp. 5–8).** If thresholds M_i satisfy ∏_i F_i(M_i)≥1−α, and a known surrogate distribution F'_i multiplicatively approximates the tails 1−F_i(x) for x≤M_i within 1±ε, then Proposition 1.2 gives a policy based on F' with ratio C*(1−α)(1−ε)² on the true instance. Proposition 1.5 is the minimax step: for bounded variables, a randomized policy achieves C* and reaches each variable i with probability at least C*. Its proof introduces nonnegative dual penalties b_i, uses rare Bernoulli spike perturbations to force reach-probability constraints, then applies Sion's minimax theorem. Proposition 1.3 truncates the upper tails and Lemma 1.4 lower-bounds reward on the event some variable exceeds its cutoff.
2. **Replace many small distributions by an iid surrogate (Proposition 1.7, pp. 9–12).** A distribution is ε-small when P[X_i=0]≥1−ε. If s≥n/2 variables are ε-small and ε>1/√n, replace them by iid draws from their geometric-mean CDF G=(∏_{i≤s}F_i)^(1/s). For any deterministic threshold policy and permutation, after independently permuting those s variables, expected reward falls by at most O(ε)E[max_i X_i]. Lemma 1.8 compares survival probabilities under a uniform permutation; Lemma 1.9 compares expected threshold-truncated values to the geometric mean; Lemma 1.10 controls the product after omitting one factor. Linearity extends the comparison to randomized policies and random, possibly correlated arrival orders.
3. **Estimate from samples (Propositions 1.11–1.14 and Theorem 1.1, pp. 13–16).** The sample scheme estimates a low quantile T of the maximum, identifies a small exceptional set L of non-ε-small variables, replaces the many remaining variables by the geometric-mean iid surrogate, and estimates both the surrogate and exceptional distributions in multiplicative tail distance. The proof uses Chernoff and DKW bounds, with O(ε⁻⁵) samples per variable overall, then composes Propositions 1.2 and 1.7.
4. **Polynomial-time policy computation (Proposition 1.15, pp. 16–18).** Exponential state-space LPs encode randomized threshold policies with the needed payoff and reach-probability constraints. Once all but a constant number of distributions are iid and supports are polynomial, symmetrizing the LP collapses equivalent states to polynomially many; the resulting LP yields a polynomial-time policy. The free-order version uses an analogous LP over order/state pairs.

The proof's broader claim includes the iid variant, and it separately notes the 0.745−ε guarantee for prophet secretary when all variables are ε-small (using the referenced full-information result). Those are not the same as Theorem 1.1's general heterogeneous prophet-secretary ratio C_S−ε.

## Boundaries for transfer

This is a sample-complexity and existence/computation result for single-choice independent variables. It supplies no explicit numerical C_S or C_F, no adversarial-order guarantee, and no matching/OCRS conclusion. Proposition 1.7 needs its stated smallness and size hypotheses; it is not a blanket replacement of arbitrary variables by iid ones. The polynomial-time LP guarantee depends on finite empirical supports and only a constant number of exceptional distributions.
