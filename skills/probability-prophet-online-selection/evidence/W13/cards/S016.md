## Definitions and reusable framework

For a downward-closed feasibility family `F⊆2^E` and activation vector `x∈P`, an OCRS sees independently active elements in some order and chooses a feasible subset. An **S-OCRS** is stationary: the law of the selected feasible set is the same for every arrival permutation. Selectability α means `P[e is selected]≥α x_e` for every e. The paper characterizes implementability through a stationary OCRS linear program and gives a universal simulate-then-replace algorithm (Proposition 2, §§3.1–3.2, pp. 15–19).

Algorithm 1 (p. 19) samples a simulated feasible set `Ŝ~µ`. On arrival e, set `T=Ŝ\{e}`. If e is active, include it in T with conditional probability `q_e(T)/x_e`, where `q_e(T)=P_{S~µ}[e∈S | S\{e}=T]`; otherwise reject it. Then set `Ŝ←T`. The per-state implementability condition is `q_e(T)≤x_e`. Induction over arrivals shows the simulated state has law µ after each replacement, hence output law µ independently of the arrival order. This factor is why the method needs the conditional constraint, not merely correct marginal probabilities.

For the maximum-entropy instantiation, choose target marginals `p=αx` and maximize entropy over distributions on F. For p in the relative interior, the unique maximizer is Gibbs:
`µ*(S)=Z(w)^(−1)∏_{e∈S}w_e`, `w_e>0`.
Writing `ρ_e=w_e/(1+w_e)`, Lemma 1 (pp. 20–21) gives `P[e∈S | S\{e}=T]=ρ_e` if T+e is feasible and zero otherwise. Thus Algorithm 2 accepts an active, addable e by a fresh Bernoulli coin with probability `ρ_e/x_e`; if inactive or not addable it rejects, and it updates the simulated state either way. The coin is required even when e is legal to add. Since `p_e=P(Add(e))ρ_e=αx_e`, the stationarity constraint `ρ_e≤x_e` follows if `P(Add(e))≥α`. Therefore the main structural proof obligation becomes the **addability bound** under the Gibbs law with marginals αx.

## Main selectability theorems

- **Theorem 1 (non-bipartite graph matchings, p. 23):** for every fractional matching x, a Gibbs maximum-entropy distribution and Algorithm 2 give a 1/3-selectable S-OCRS. For an edge e={u,v}, failure of addability is covered by either endpoint being matched. Under a matching, incident selected-edge events are disjoint, so `P[Matched(z)]=Σ_{f∋z}p_f=αΣ_{f∋z}x_f≤α`; a union bound gives `P[Add(e)]≥1−2α`, which reaches α at α=1/3. The paper notes the same argument gives `1/(L+1)` for rank-L hypergraph matchings (Appendix EC.5.3).
- **Theorem 2 (bipartite matchings, pp. 23–25):** α*=`(3−√5)/2≈0.381966` is attained by this S-OCRS and is optimal among all S-OCRSs. The lower bound uses positive correlation of matched events on opposite sides under the bipartite monomer-dimer Gibbs measure (Lemma 2; proof in EC.5.4): `P[Add(e)]≥P[unmatched(u),unmatched(v)]≥(1−P[Matched(u)])(1−P[Matched(v)])≥(1−α)^2`. Set `(1−α)^2=α`. The upper bound comes from a stationary-LP counterexample (Lemma 3, proof EC.5.5), not only from a limitation of this particular algorithm.
- **Theorem 3 (k-uniform matroid, pp. 26–28):** the optimal S-OCRS selectability is `α_k=P[Q<k | Q≤k]`, where `Q~Pois(k)`. Its asymptotic form is `1−sqrt(2/(πk))+O(1/k)`; the square root was checked in the rendered p. 26 (the OCR transcription drops the radical). Under the Gibbs law, S has the distribution of independent Bernoulli indicators `Y_e~Ber(ρ_e)` conditioned on `W=ΣY_e≤k`. Feasibility gives `E[W|W≤k]=Σp_e=α_kΣx_e≤kα_k`; the Poisson identity `E[Q|Q≤k]=kP[Q<k|Q≤k]=kα_k` and Lemma 4's truncated-Poisson comparison yield `P[|S|<k]≥α_k`, which implies addability for every e. Lemma 5's symmetric instances as `|E|→∞` prove the matching upper bound for all S-OCRSs.
- **Theorem 4 (capacity-k rank-L hypergraph matching / NRM, pp. 28–29):** let `Q~Pois((1−ε)k)`, `β=P[Q=k|Q≤k]`, and `γ=(1−ε)(1−β)`. The specified Gibbs law with `ρ_e=γx_e` gives a `γ(1−Lβ)`-selectable S-OCRS. For `k>2 log L`, choosing `ε=sqrt(2 log L/k)` gives `1−sqrt(2 log L/k)−O(k^(−1/2))`. The radical and order term were checked against p. 29 and proof EC.5.8 (pp. 72–74), as OCR loses the radical. The addability step bounds each vertex's probability of being at capacity by β, then unions over at most L vertices of an edge. A separate ordinary OCRS obtained by thinning and intersecting vertex schemes is also described, but it is not necessarily stationary.
- **Other results:** weakly-Rayleigh matroids have a 1/2-selectable S-OCRS (EC.2); knapsack has 1/4 (EC.3); general downward-closed constraints on N elements have a `1/(2√N+1)` S-OCRS and a matching-order `O(1/√N)` upper-bound family (EC.4, EC.5.6). These require the distinct constructions and assumptions in the corresponding appendices.

## Proof and scope boundaries

The reusable-product application (Section 7, pp. 29–36) starts from independent homogeneous Poisson arrivals of request types, with known rates/rewards and independently exponential usage durations of known means in the formal model; the authors state that the admission-rule lemma extends to general duration distributions with those means. A static fluid relaxation bounds the long-run offline benchmark. An n-split and stationary OCRS round the proposals into feasible admissions, with an ε loss in the general lemma. This is a reusable-resource reduction; it does not identify its benchmark or assumptions with the single-use prophet benchmark. The assortment extension is also separately modeled.

Stationarity means permutation-invariance of the output-set distribution, which is stronger than merely guaranteeing per-element marginals for every order. For Gibbs-based constructions the conditional accept coin `ρ_e/x_e` remains essential. The bipartite optimality claim is only for S-OCRSs; their general matching discussion explicitly leaves optimality open. The k-uniform theorem is a finite-k exact formula plus stated asymptotic expansion; do not replace it by the looser `1−2/(πk)` OCR rendering. For capacity-k rank-L matchings, the asymptotic theorem is `1−sqrt(2 log L/k)−O(k^(−1/2))`, not `1−2 log L/k−O(k^(−1/2))`.
