## Scope and central method

The tutorial studies sequential randomized rounding (SRR): first solve a fluid/expectation LP, then produce an online policy whose actual marginal actions track the LP while respecting sequential constraints. The paper illustrates how state probability variables, basic-solution rounding, or OCRSs make this possible. Each factor below has its own model and LP denominator; “LP-relative” does not mean relative to an integral offline optimum.

## Theorem-bearing cases

### Online Bayesian selection and OCRS (Section 2)

For the fixed-order rationing problem, each agent i independently needs a unit with probability \(x_i\), and no more than k units are available. A fair policy must offer each agent with the same probability \(\gamma\), and supply meets expected demand \(\sum_i x_i\le k\).

- **Theorem 1 (PDF p. 5):** for k=1 and order 1,…,n the optimal common offer probability is \(\gamma_1(x)=1/(1+\sum_{i<n}x_i)\). The upper bound follows because the final agent can be offered only if no earlier offer is accepted; the matching policy offers i with independent coin probability \(\gamma/(1-\gamma\sum_{j<i}x_j)\), and induction gives every agent marginal \(\gamma\).
- **Theorem 2 (Jiang et al., cited result; LP (6), PDF pp. 6–7):** for general k, the optimal \(\gamma\) is the value of a state-flow LP over \(\beta_i^\ell\), the probability of \(\ell\) units remaining before agent i, and \(\alpha_i^\ell\), the unconditional probability of offering in that state. The state recursion is coupled across \(\ell\), and an optimal solution implements Alaei's rule. This is a cited theorem presented in the tutorial; the proof of the external result is not reproduced as a new result of this paper.
- **Theorem 3 (Lee–Singla, cited result; PDF pp. 8–9):** for k=1, \(\sum_i x_i\le1\), and a uniformly random order, timestamp each item with iid \(U_i\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]\) and offer to i with independent probability \(e^{-U_i x_i}\) if supply remains. Then every agent is offered with probability at least \(1-1/e\). Conditional on \(U_i=t\), independence gives an integrand \(e^{-t x_i}\prod_{j\ne i}(1-\int_0^t e^{-u x_j}x_j\,du)\), which is bounded below by \(e^{-t\sum_jx_j}\); integrate over [0,1].
- **Theorem 4 (cited OCRS/ex-ante result; PDF p. 9):** for \(\sum_i x_i\le k\), uniformly random order, and k-unit capacity, there is a policy offering each agent with probability at least \(1-e^{-k}k^k/k!\). This is the exact worst-case random-order factor \(\gamma_k^{RO}\); for k=2 it is \(1-2e^{-2}\approx0.729\). The theorem does not give an explicit policy for general k. It is a fairness/offer-selectability guarantee, not directly a prophet reward ratio.

The general OCRS discussion in §2.3 (PDF pp. 10–11) distinguishes fixed-order and random-order selectability and notes that “greedy” properties are needed for guarantees against an almighty adversary. Preserve the chosen adversary model.

### Sequential hiring (Section 3)

Candidate i has known weight \(w_i\) and independently accepts an offer with probability \(p_i\). At most T offers may be made and at most k candidates hired. LP (7) maximizes \(\sum_iw_ip_iy_i\) subject to \(\sum_i y_i\le T\), \(\sum_i p_i y_i\le k\), and \(0\le y_i\le1\). Lemma 1 (PDF p. 11) shows this LP upper-bounds every policy because the offer decision is independent of whether the candidate accepts.

**Theorem 5 (PDF pp. 11–13):** Algorithm 3 solves LP (7), rounds its basic optimum (at most two fractional coordinates) while preserving the offer budget, then offers in decreasing weight order. Its expected value is at least
\[
\left(1-e^{-k}\frac{k^k}{k!}\right)\mathrm{OPT}_{LP}.
\]
For k=1 the source gives a self-contained proof: the two fractional coordinates yield negative correlation of rejection events; hence for every j, \(\Pr(\text{none of }1,\ldots,j\text{ accepts})\le\prod_{i\le j}(1-p_i y_i)\le e^{-\sum_{i\le j}p_i y_i}\). The layer-cake decomposition by \(w_j-w_{j+1}\), together with \(\sum_i p_i y_i\le1\), yields ratio at least (1-1/e). The general-k factor is derived in the cited Epstein–Ma correlation-gap argument, not proved by the displayed k=1 derivation.

### Stochastic knapsack (Section 4)

There are T periods and independent jobs i with random weight-duration pairs \((W_i,D_i)\), independent across jobs but possibly correlated within a pair. A job must be started without observing its weight or duration; processing is nonpreemptive, one job at a time, and a reward is earned only if it completes by T. Assumption 1 requires durations to be positive integers within the horizon. LP (17) uses \(y_{it}\) for the probability job i starts at t, expected active-job capacity constraints, and objective \(\sum_{i,t}\mathbb E[W_i1\{D_i\le T-t+1\}]y_{it}\). Lemma 2 (PDF p. 18) proves \(\mathrm{OPT}_{LP}\) upper-bounds any policy.

**Theorem 6 (PDF p. 18):** Algorithm 4 starts job i at time t with probability exactly \(y_{it}/2\), so its value equals \(\mathrm{OPT}_{LP}/2\). It computes \(\mathrm{Free}(i,t)\), the probability that no job is running and i has not previously started, then samples with probability proportional to \(y_{it}/(2\mathrm{Free}(i,t))\). Induction gives the exact marginals; a union bound and LP feasibility show the sampling vector has total mass at most 1. This is not the unit-density A005 model.

### Stochastic matching (Section 5.1)

Agents arrive in a fixed order and independently materialize with probability \(p_i\); each resource can be matched once, with reward \(w_{ji}\). LP (19) has resource constraints \(\sum_i x_{ji}\le1\), agent constraints \(\sum_jx_{ji}\le p_i\), and upper-bounds any policy.

**Theorem 7 (PDF pp. 20–21):** run an independent k=1 OCRS with \(\gamma=1/2\) for every resource and, conditional on agent i materializing, sample resource j with probability \(x_{ji}/p_i\) (otherwise choose no resource). Each i,j match occurs with probability exactly \(x_{ji}/2\), hence value is \(\mathrm{OPT}_{LP}/2\). The factor is tight in the model: one resource and two agents with \((p_1,w_1)=(1,1)\), \((p_2,w_2)=(\varepsilon,1/\varepsilon)\) make LP value at least \(2-\varepsilon\) while any online policy gets 1 (PDF p. 21). The later sections on IID matching, probing, and general matching polytopes are related applications with their own assumptions; they are not extensions of this theorem without checking those models.

## Transfer boundary

Section 2's random-order guarantee assumes a uniform random permutation; Theorem 1 and fixed-order results use a pre-specified order. Theorem 5 is against its hiring LP, Theorem 6 against the time-indexed stochastic-knapsack LP, and Theorem 7 against the matching LP. Keep candidate acceptances, job durations, materialization probabilities, capacity state, and each benchmark separate when using these as methods for OCRS or resource-allocation problems.
