## Degree-two Gaussian-polynomial anti-concentration

Theorem 1.2 (PDF p. 1) states: if `X∼N(0,I_n)` and `f:R^n→R` is a nonnegative degree-two polynomial, then for every `ε>0`,

`P{ f(X) ≤ ε E[f(X)] } ≤ (2e ε)^(1/2)`.

The paper uses “degree two” in its ordinary exact-degree sense. If a user wants to include the zero polynomial (or reads “degree two” as degree at most two), the positive-mean qualification `E f(X)>0` must be stated: for `f≡0` the displayed event has probability one, contradicting the bound for small `ε`. Constants `f≡c>0` cause no issue. The broader Carbery–Wright theorem recalled as Theorem 1.1 (PDF p. 1) is only quoted, not proved here: it assumes a log-concave law `μ` on `R^n`, a degree-`d` polynomial, and gives `P{|f(X)|≤ε E|f(X)|}≤C d ε^(1/d)`.

## Proof certificate and dependencies

Every nonnegative quadratic polynomial is represented by

`f(x)=[1;x]^T Q [1;x]`, `Q=[[q11,q12^T],[q12,Q22]]⪰0`,

with `E f(X)=q11+tr(Q22)=tr(Q)` (PDF pp. 1–2). Lemma 2.1 (p. 2) completes the square in the Gaussian integral and uses the nonnegative Schur complement to obtain, for `λ>0`,

`E exp(−λ f(X)) ≤ det(I_n+2λQ22)^(−1/2)`.

Lemma 2.2 (PDF pp. 2–3) uses eigenvalues `λ_i(Q22)≥0`, hence `det(I+2ηQ22)=∏_i(1+2ηλ_i)≥1+2η tr(Q22)`, then Chernoff with `η=(1−ε)/(2ε tr(Q22))` to show

`P{f(X)≤ε tr(Q22)}≤(e ε)^(1/2)`.

Lemma 2.3 (PDF pp. 3–4) proves that for `Y∼N(μ,σ²)`, `μ∈R`, `σ>0`, and `ε∈(0,1)`, `P{|Y|≤ε|μ|}≤ε/2`. It writes this probability as `h(r,σ;ε)` with `r=|μ|`, optimizes over `r/σ` at `r*=σ sqrt(log((1+ε)/(1−ε))/(2ε))`, and uses convexity of the resulting envelope `ζ(ε)` and its endpoint limits to obtain `ζ(ε)≤ε/2`.

Lemma 2.4 (PDF pp. 4–5) splits on `q11≤tr(Q22)` or `q11>tr(Q22)`. In the first case it applies Lemma 2.2 with `2ε`. In the second it adds `γ>0` to `q11`, completes the square using `Q22−q12(q11+γ)^(−1)q12^T⪰0`, applies Lemma 2.3, and lets a decreasing sequence `γ_k↓0` pass through continuity from below. This yields the theorem's `(2e ε)^(1/2)` factor. If `tr(Q22)=0`, positive semidefiniteness forces `q12=0`; the second case and limiting argument cover the nonzero constant component. For the zero polynomial the theorem needs the qualification above.

## Transfer boundary

This is a small-ball/anti-concentration estimate for one nonnegative quadratic polynomial of a standard isotropic Gaussian vector, normalized by its expectation. It is not a tail bound for an arbitrary polynomial, nor does it directly cover non-isotropic Gaussian input without an explicit linear transformation and a check that nonnegativity and degree are preserved. The note's proof does not establish the full Carbery–Wright theorem; Theorem 1.1 is cited background.
