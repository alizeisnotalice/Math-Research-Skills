## Theorem and influence normalization

The domain is the uniform Boolean cube (\Omega_n=\{0,1\}^n), (f:\Omega_n\to\{0,1\}), with normalized Walsh coefficients. Write (p=\|f\|_2^2=\mathbb E f). **Theorem 1.1 (physical p.1; statement continues on p.2)** assumes

\[
\sum_{|S|>1}\widehat f(S)^2\le\delta.
\]

Then either (p<K'\delta), or (p>1-K'\delta), or there is a coordinate (i) for which (\|f-x_i\|_2^2\le K\delta), or (\|f-(1-x_i)\|_2^2\le K\delta), where (K,K'>0) are absolute. Thus the conclusion is concentration near a constant in the two extreme-density cases, and otherwise near a dictator or its complement; for 0/1-valued functions, squared (L_2) distance (for example, (\|f-x_i\|_2^2)) equals normalized Hamming distance (\Pr[f\ne x_i]); the unsquared (L_2) distance is its square root.

**Corollary 1.2 (physical p.2):** for (A\subseteq\Omega_n), (|A|\le2^{n-1}), if its edge boundary (E(A)\le(1+\epsilon)|A|), then for some coordinate half-cube (A_i=\{x:x_i=0\}) or its complement, (\mathbb P(A\triangle A_i)\le K\epsilon) or (\mathbb P(A\triangle\overline{A_i})\le K\epsilon). Under the paper’s standard normalized Walsh coefficients for (1_A), (I(A):=E(A)/2^{n-1}=4\sum_S|S|\widehat{1_A}(S)^2); this is total flip influence / average sensitivity / edge boundary per (2^{n-1}). Preserve that normalization when comparing to (\sum_i\Pr[f(x)\ne f(x^{\oplus i})]).

## Proofs and dependencies

- **Proof I, §2 (physical pp.3–7):** Proposition 2.1 chooses a weighted-majority Boolean function maximizing the first-level Fourier inner product for fixed weights (t_i), since the objective is a signed sum over points with coefficient (T-2\sum_{i\in S}t_i). With (t_i=\widehat f(\{i\})), this yields a bound involving the (L_1/L_2) norms of the Rademacher-sum vector. Proposition 2.2 proves that if a normalized Rademacher sum has small variance of its absolute value, its largest coefficient must be near 1. The first proof invokes the König–Schütt–Tomczak-Jaegermann estimate; an elementary Chernoff-tail proof is also given for small error. Together, near-maximal low-level mass forces all but one first-level coefficient to be small. The source states this argument at a high level and gives the full auxiliary estimates in §2.
- **Proof II, §3 (physical pp.8–11):** Lemma 3.1: for (f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}) with high-degree squared mass (\epsilon=\sum_{|T|>1}\widehat f(T)^2), there are (i) and a one-coordinate affine function (D_i) with
  \[
  \|f-D_i\|_2^2<\left(1+\frac{17496}{1/4-\epsilon}\right)\epsilon.
  \]
  The displayed denominator requires (\epsilon<1/4); that positivity range is implicit in the printed statement and must be retained when applying the bound. Balance is obtained by adjoining (y) and setting (g(x,0)=f(x)), (g(x,1)=1-f(1-x)), preserving the high-level tail. For the balanced case, write (S=\sum_{|T|\le1}\widehat f(T)u_T), (L=f-S), and (R=S^2-S). The Fourier coefficients satisfy \(\widehat R(\emptyset)=-\epsilon\), \(\widehat R(i)=0\), and \(\widehat R(ij)=2\widehat f(i)\widehat f(j)\). Lemma 3.2 gives \(\mathbb E R^2\le8748\epsilon\): first \(\Pr(|R|>\alpha)\le16\epsilon/\alpha^2\), then degree-2 Bonami–Beckner gives \(\mathbb E R^4\le81(\mathbb E R^2)^2\); optimizing at \(\alpha=54\sqrt\epsilon\) yields the bound. Corollary 3.3 lower-bounds a maximal first-level coefficient using \(\sum_i\widehat f(i)^2=1/4-\epsilon\), and gives the one-coordinate approximant.

The social-choice **Theorem 1.3** on physical p.2 is stated as an application and explicitly referred to Kalai (2001) for proof; it is not proved in this paper. The Fourier stability theorem and edge-boundary corollary are proved here.

## Scope boundary

This is a near-first-two-level stability theorem. It assumes small **squared Fourier (L_2) tail above level 1**, and its conclusion is dictator/constant closeness. It does not say that merely having nonzero singleton coefficients forces quadratic Fourier support, and it does not identify first-level mass with coordinate dependence. It is not an influence theorem under low maximum influence.
