## Model and definitions

The graph is infinite, connected, locally finite, and quasi-transitive; the paper usually assumes it simple. An n-step SAW visits no vertex twice. The connective constant is defined from supremal counts over starting vertices, with submultiplicativity giving a limit; on a quasi-transitive graph the rooted exponential rate agrees at every fixed root.

A graph height function is a pair (h,H), with integer-valued h, H acting quasi-transitively, h having H-invariant increments, and every vertex having neighbors both above and below. The finite height increment is d(h). For multiple H-orbits an orbit connector length r is used. The action is unimodular exactly where the bridge theorem requires it. A bridge satisfies h(π0)<h(πi)≤h(πn) for i≥1; a half-space SAW only requires h(π0)<h(πi). The bridge constant depends on the height data until the equality theorem applies.

## Precisely stated conclusions

**Theorem 4.3 (p.11; proof §§6–7, pp.15–23).** If the graph is infinite, connected, quasi-transitive, locally finite and simple, and possesses a unimodular graph height function, then its bridge constant β equals its SAW connective constant μ. Unimodularity is material: Remark 4.4, p.11 gives a regular tree with a non-unimodular horocyclic height whose bridge constant is 1 while μ=2.

**Theorem 5.1 (p.12).** Fix G in the class of infinite, connected, quasi-transitive, locally finite, simple rooted graphs. For integers D≥1 and R≥0, let G_{D,R} consist of graphs possessing a unimodular height function with d(h)≤D and r(h,H)≤R. If G_n∈G_{D,R} and the rooted balls agree to radii K(G,G_n)→∞, then μ(G_n)→μ(G). The limiting graph G itself is only assumed to lie in the stated rooted graph class; the theorem does not require an assigned height function on G.

**Theorem 5.2 (p.13).** For a transitive G with a transitive subgroup Γ, normal subgroups A_n⊲Γ with minimum nonzero displacement Δ(A_n)→∞, and quotients G/A_n∈G_{D,0}, the connective constants of those quotient graphs tend to μ(G).

The proof of Theorem 5.1 uses Proposition 5.4 (pp.13–14). Its upper comparison matches counts inside the common rooted ball and uses submultiplicativity. Its lower comparison uses β(G′)=μ(G′) and a bridge-unwrapping loss exp(B/√(K−L)); B depends on D,R and a uniform degree bound, and L bounds the orbit count. These controls explain why rooted-ball agreement alone is not the hypothesis.

## Proof mechanism and constants

In the transitive case, Hammersley–Welsh unfolding gives c_n≤exp(A√(d n)) b_n (Proposition 6.2, pp.15–19). The proof decomposes a half-space SAW into bridge/reversed-bridge pieces with strictly decreasing integer spans. Unimodularity enters through mass transport, equating the relevant automorphism-image multiplicities (Lemmas 6.3–6.4, pp.15–16). Surgery combines the first two or three span pieces; its multiplicities are controlled by those lemmas, and the number of distinct partitions of total span T contributes at most exp(O(√T)), using Hardy–Ramanujan (pp.15, 17–19). A mass-transport split at the last minimum-height vertex then gives σ_n≤exp(B√(d(n+1))) b_{n+1} (Proposition 6.6, pp.19–20). Taking n-th roots removes the subexponential factor and yields μ≤β; β≤μ is immediate.

For quasi-transitive H, the proof inserts bounded-length orbit connectors and tracks orbit-type multiplicities; extra factors are absorbed in exp(A√n) with A=A(d,r,δ_G) (Propositions 7.1 and 7.4, pp.20–23). No universal numeric A independent of height increment, connector length, and degree is asserted.
