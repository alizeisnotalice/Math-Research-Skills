## Model and theorem

The graph X is infinite, connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive, simple, and has no loops or multiple edges. A graph height function (h,Γ) is integer-valued, Γ-difference-invariant, Γ acts quasi-transitively, and every vertex has neighbors of strictly lower and higher height. The height increment per edge is bounded by d. A bridge has h(v0)<h(vi)≤h(vn) for i≥1; a reversed bridge uses the opposite inequalities. Their exponential rates are β(X,h) and β(X,−h).

**Theorem 3.2 (p.5).** For every graph height function on such a graph,
μ(X)=max{β(X,h),β(X,−h)}.
No unimodularity is assumed. The earlier one-direction identity μ=β(X,h) is restated as Theorem 3.1 under unimodularity; equality of the two directions is not generally available.

## Proof chain and quantitative loss

In the transitive case, a half-space SAW is decomposed into alternating bridge and reversed-bridge segments with strictly decreasing positive integer spans a₁>…>a_k. For fixed spans, odd and even segments are separately concatenated into increasing and decreasing bridges; the original walk is reconstructed from the pair, giving the injection in Lemma 3.3 (pp.6–7). The number of distinct-span partitions is controlled by P_D(A), with log P_D(A)~π√(A/3) (3.5). Since span≤dn, Lemma 3.4 gives
h_n ≤ P_D(dn) Σ_{m=0}^n b_m b̄_{n−m} ≤ K exp(B√n) β_max^n
for any B>π√(d/3) after increasing K.

To control all SAWs, split at the last index of minimum height and prepend a lower-height neighbor, producing two half-space walks; c_n≤Σ_{l=0}^n h_{n−l}h_{l+1}. The convolution costs an exponent threshold C>π√(2d/3), yielding c_n≤exp(C√(n+1))β_max^(n+1) for sufficiently large n (Lemma 3.5, p.8). Taking n-th roots and using μ^n≤c_n proves μ≤β_max; the reverse inequality is immediate.

For quasi-transitive actions, the proof uses finitely many orbit types and connector bridges of length at most r. Lemma 3.6 inserts connectors when concatenating segments; at most (r+1)^(k−1) connector choices are lost. Lemmas 3.7–3.8 absorb these factors into changed constants B,C while retaining exp(O(√n)) loss (pp.9–11). Constants depend on graph/height parameters; they are not universal numeric error bounds.

## Grandparent-graph example

Section 4 (pp.11–14) takes the 3-regular tree oriented toward a fixed end, adds edges from each vertex to its grandparent, and uses the layer height. Its automorphism action is transitive but non-unimodular: stabilizer orbit sizes for a vertex and predecessor differ. Increasing-bridge generating functions have convergence threshold at the smallest positive root of
1−8x+10x²−8x³+8x⁴,
so β(X,h)≈6.64993. Reversed bridges use
1−4x+3x²−x³+x⁴,
so β(X,−h)≈3.10380. Theorem 3.2 gives μ(X)=max(...)≈6.64993. Rendered pp.13–14 confirm both quartics and rounded constants.

## Method use and transfer limits

- Provisional skill use: the general two-direction bridge theorem and explicit bridge-generating-function analysis on deterministic quasi-transitive graph SAW models.
- C107: an exponential-growth identity for counts does not prove tightness of planar SAW curves, a scaling limit, a discrete DMP, or SLE(8/3) identification.
- C108: a random infinite percolation cluster is not covered merely because each realization is locally finite. The theorem needs a deterministic graph with a quasi-transitive action and graph height function; it establishes no quenched almost-sure limit or starting-point independence on the random cluster.
