# PEM1991 — choosing a spanning tree for the integer lattice uniformly

**Source:** Robin Pemantle, *Choosing a Spanning Tree for the Integer Lattice Uniformly*, Annals of Probability 19(4) (1991), 1559–1574, DOI 10.1214/aop/1176990223. Full reading of the 24-page local copy, arXiv:math/0404043v1 (posted 2 Apr. 2004); the PDF front matter itself is dated 20 Aug. 2018. The article is the 1991 paper, not a 2018 result.

## Limit law and tail field

- **Theorem 2.3 (PDF pp. 6–8).** Uniform spanning trees on any edge-set exhaustion of ℤ^d, (d\ge2), converge weakly to the same translation-invariant law μ on spanning forests. The proof first checks monotonic convergence of finite cylinder probabilities using contraction/deletion and Rayleigh monotonicity, then establishes acyclicity, spanning, and translation invariance.
- **Theorem 2.4 (PDF pp. 8–10).** The limiting forest has a trivial tail σ-field. The proof conditions on a tail event outside a large box, sandwiches the conditional probability of a local edge cylinder between the finite free and wired electrical boundary versions, and shows those probabilities converge together by coupling random walks until they cover the finitely many relevant edges. This proves independence of the tail event from every finite cylinder.

## Finite-volume construction used by the original paper

- **Section 2 and Lemma 2.1 (PDF pp. 2–5).** The paper predates Wilson’s algorithm. Its finite-box construction starts simple random walk at a root and runs it until the walk covers the finite graph; it retains the edge by which each newly visited vertex is first reached. This random-walk spanning-tree construction (the Aldous–Broder construction) has the uniform spanning-tree law. The paper also compares it with an electrical recursive construction in Lemma 2.2. Do not attribute this 1991 proof to Wilson (1996).

## LERW intersection input

- **Lemma 3.1 (PDF pp. 11–13).** An independent LERW from (v) and SRW from (w) intersect infinitely often a.s. for (d=3,4). For (d\ge5), they intersect only finitely often and the probability of any intersection is between constants times ​\(|v-w|^{4-d}\). The upper bound uses a second moment estimate for the number (X) of intersection points; the lower bound follows from (\mathbb P(X>0)\ge(\mathbb EX)^2/\mathbb EX^2). To bound ​\(\mathbb EX\) below, first show (\mathbb P(x\in\mathrm{LERW}_v)\ge c|v-x|^{2-d}\) by stopping the original walk at its first visit to (x) and bounding away from zero the probability that the past segment avoids an independent walk; then sum against the Green function. Lemmas 3.2–3.4 (PDF pp. 13–17) provide the backward-loop-erasure bijection and uniform convergence to infinite-volume LERW needed by the main proof.

## Component geometry

- **Theorem 4.2 (PDF pp. 18–21).** The limit law is connected a.s. for (d=3,4); for (d\ge5) it has infinitely many components a.s. Moreover, for distinct (v,w), the probability they lie in the same component is between positive dimension-dependent constants times ​\(|v-w|^{4-d}\). The proof expresses the finite-tree path event through the random-walk tree and backward loop-erasure: conditional on a walk from \(v\) hitting \(w\) before the boundary, the tree path between \(v,w\) is the loop-erasure of that walk segment (Lemma 4.1). Decompose the covering walk at the first boundary/hit event and use Lemmas 3.3–3.4 to pass the backward-loop-erasure pieces to infinite-volume LERW. The limiting connection probability becomes the probability that an independent LERW from \(w\) intersects SRW from \(v\). Lemma 3.1 gives the dimension dichotomy and the \(|v-w|^{4-d}\) decay. Translation invariance plus tail triviality makes the component count deterministic; a first-moment count of connected pairs in a box rules out any finite component count for (d\ge5). Wilson’s algorithm is not used as the source’s finite-volume construction.
- **Theorem 4.3 (PDF pp. 22–23).** For (d\le4), the spanning tree has one topological end a.s. Stationarity/ergodicity and Burton–Keane exclude positive-density branch points, so there are at most two ends. If there were two, the separating vertices would have positive density; three far-separated vertices then force one to separate the other two with probability bounded away from zero, contradicting the vanishing probability that a remote vertex separates the other pair. The latter estimate follows by the finite-box random-walk construction and the LERW/SRW intersection lemma.

The abstract also reports that for (d\ge5) every component has one or two ends; this card does not use that abstract claim as a separate theorem-level proof beyond the explicit statements above.
