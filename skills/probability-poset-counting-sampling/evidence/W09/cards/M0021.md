## SIS estimator and exact target

For a finite poset P, repeatedly delete a currently maximal element. Reversing the deletion list gives a linear extension. If M_t is the current maximal set and r(v)>0 is the supplied importance function, choose v in M_t with probability r(v)/r(M_t), where r(M_t) is the sum of its weights. The estimator for the resulting extension lambda is

\[
f_P(\lambda)=\prod_t\frac{r(M_t)}{r(v_t)}.
\]

The path probability is the reciprocal, 1/f_P(lambda). Therefore each linear extension contributes exactly one to the estimator's expectation, and E[f_P]=e(P) (Section 3, PDF pp. 3–5). Algorithm 1 takes O(n^2) time in the paper's implementation model; it outputs a random estimate, not the exact count and not a uniform exact sample.

## Variance results

Relative variance is RV(P)=Var(f_P)/E[f_P]^2. Theorem 4.1 (PDF pp. 5–6), not an unbiasedness theorem, states

\[
RV(P)=\left\langle\frac{f_P(\lambda)}{L}\right\rangle_u-1,\qquad L=e(P),
\]

where the average is over uniformly chosen linear extensions.

For the recurrence in Theorem 4.2 (PDF pp. 6–7), let M be the current maximal set and let L_m count deletion paths whose first choice is m. Equivalently, under the standard increasing-order convention this counts extensions ending with m; the paper's “begin with m” wording follows its deletion-path convention. Deleting the first maximal element m leaves the subposet P\setminus m, and the path weights satisfy f_P(lambda)=(r(M)/r(m)) f_{P\setminus m}(lambda'). Substitution into the second-moment identity gives

\[
RV(P)+1=\frac{r(M)}{L^2}\sum_{m\in M}\frac{L_m^2}{r(m)}
\bigl(RV(P\setminus m)+1\bigr).
\]

Theorem 4.3 (PDF pp. 7–8) defines, for posets Q of size i, with L=e(Q) and L_m the number of paths/extensions ending in maximal element m,

\[
A_i=\max_{Q:\,|Q|=i}\ \max_{m\in\max(Q)}
\frac{r(\max(Q))}{r(m)}\,\frac{L_m}{L},
\]

and Theorem 4.3 proves

\[
RV(P) \le A_1 A_2 \cdots A_n - 1.
\]

The product runs through `A_n`; the `−1` is outside the product (PDF p. 7). In the induction, the recurrence first gives `RV(P)+1 ≤ A_1⋯A_n`, using `Σ_{m∈M} L_m/L=1`, and then subtracts 1. The ideal but unavailable choice `r(m)=L_m` makes every `A_i=1` and the variance zero.

## Importance functions and reported evidence

Section 5 studies (i) r(v)=d(v), the number of descendants including v, and (ii) the changing available-spaces quotient

\[
r(v)=\frac{i+d(v)-1}{i-d(v)+1},
\]

where i is the number of elements still to be added. Descendant weights give a product lower bound; for a forest DAG the bound is exact, and the paper also describes a forest-based upper bound. The second weight was selected empirically and has no stated universal variance guarantee. Section 6 recursively factors connected components, with worst-case O(n^3) time. Section 7 reports empirical reductions in relative variance for generated random posets; those plots are evidence about those experiments only, not a worst-case bound.

## Assumptions and transfer limit

Every currently maximal element must receive positive proposal weight. The deletion order is reversed to produce the linear extension, and the estimator's target is the fixed poset count e(P). This is randomized approximate counting through an unbiased estimator; it does not provide an exact or uniform sampler. It does not directly count free-boundary square labelings conditioned on exactly two peaks.
