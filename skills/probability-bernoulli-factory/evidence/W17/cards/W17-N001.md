## Model and exact result

The input is an i.i.d. stream of Bernoulli(p) coins with unknown fixed p; the factory must output Bernoulli(f(p)) exactly. The paper gives a practical envelope-cascade implementation for strictly linear, concave, or convex factory functions on [0,1]. Its worked construction is the elbow f(p)=min(cp,1−ε), c>1, 0<ε<1, with a piecewise-linear concave target and a sequence of nested envelopes. The discussion of general concave/convex functions requires certifying that the chosen envelopes remain on the correct side of f; the paper says a numerical method may be needed (physical pp. 1, 4, 9–12, 17–22).

For n input bits, each bit string with k ones has probability p^k(1−p)^(n−k). Bernstein lower approximants to f and 1−f define disjoint terminal sets A_{n,k} (output 1) and B_{n,k} (output 0); leftover strings form C_{n,k} (continue). The approximants must stay below their targets, converge to them, and their terminal-string sets must be nested under adding bits. This makes the continuation decision a finite-set-size calculation conditional on the observed Hamming weight, without enumerating every bit string (pp. 5–7).

The central implementation improvement is to cascade upper envelopes at checkpoints m1<m2<…, choosing each new elbow so its Bernstein approximation is below the previous envelope but remains above f. At each checkpoint, add fresh input bits, remove descendants already assigned to terminal sets, and draw the terminal/continue category with probabilities proportional to the three set sizes. This preserves the exact output probabilities while permitting envelope choices tuned to the likely input-p range (pp. 9–12).

## Proof mechanism and cost evidence

Correctness rests on the Bernstein representation of bit-string probabilities, lower/upper envelopes converging to f and 1−f, and nesting of terminal sets. Concavity gives the lower-side inequality E[f(K/n)]≤f(p) (with the reverse for convex f); the cascade supplies the non-natural envelope direction. The theoretical section argues existence of convergent cascades and, by coupling to Nacu–Peres envelopes, an asymptotically fast choice. Its p. 16 footnote limits exponential-tail envelopes to small p and gives polynomial tails elsewhere. The example's fifth checkpoint is printed `n₅=2^37` (pp. 16–17), not `m₅`; it is an impractical example value, not a usable runtime recommendation.

Two concrete cost facts should be kept distinct:

- Von Neumann’s fair-coin factory rejects 00/11 and maps 01/10 to opposite outputs. For 0<p<1, the output is fair and the expected number of input bits is 1/[p(1−p)] (physical p. 3).
- For f(p)=min(2p,0.8), p=0.01, the paper’s 10,000-run comparison reports 20 minimum bits, mean 66 and SD 512 for the cascade, versus 256 minimum bits, mean 562.9 and SD 2104.6 for the cited alternative (Table 3, physical p. 15). These are specific simulation results, not a general complexity theorem.

In the linear case, at most one continuation string occurs per Hamming weight, giving the paper’s tail bound P(T>n)≤Σ_{i=0}^n p^i(1−p)^(n−i) (p. 7). This is pointwise in an interior p; the article does not establish a uniform-in-p stopping-moment characterization. The von Neumann example itself does not terminate at p=0,1. Do not infer arbitrary-function support or an iff for finite moments from the cascade examples.

## Relevance boundary

Useful for designing exact scalar factories from Bernstein envelopes and for distinguishing practical finite-stage cost evidence from asymptotic existence. It does not classify all r-th stopping moments for arbitrary f, nor give a general bound for the growth of checkpoints or envelope computation. Use the K&O boundary condition only as a cited background theorem, not as a new theorem proved here.
