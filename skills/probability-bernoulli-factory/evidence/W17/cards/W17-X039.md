## Scalar Bernoulli-factory model and exact subroutines (§2, physical pp. 4–5)

Definition 2.1 takes a scalar function `f:(0,1)→(0,1)`, independent access to a `p`-coin with unknown `p∈(0,1)`, and requires one output `f(p)`-coin. The stated open domain does not include deterministic endpoint coins. The paper’s examples include `p²` by two tosses and `exp(p−1)` by drawing `K~Poisson(1)` and outputting 1 iff all `K` coin flips are 1.

Section 2 also lists constructions with explicit domains/costs: down-scaling `λp` for `λ∈[0,1]` by multiplying an independent known-`λ` coin with a `p`-coin; doubling `2p` only for `p≤1/2−δ`, with expected `O(1/δ)` input tosses; and Bernoulli addition on `p_1+p_2≤1−δ` by averaging followed by doubling. Doubling at `p=1/2` cannot use a finite stopping rule: a deterministic output 1 conflicts with the positive probability of every finite binary history under a fair input. The probability-generating-function construction draws `K` from a known nonnegative-integer law and returns 1 iff all K tosses are heads; its expected input toss count is `E K`. For `K~Poisson(λ)`, this gives `exp(λ(p−1))` with expected λ tosses.

For the “expectations from samples” model (Definition 3.1, physical p. 5), input distributions are independently sampleable, supported on `[0,1]`, and have means `μ∈(0,1)^n`; the requested output is a sample from a finite-outcome law depending on those means. The Continuous-to-Bernoulli step draws `Z∈[0,1]` and then a Bernoulli(`Z`) outcome, producing a coin with bias `E Z`. This modeling bridge depends on the paper’s access to internal randomization.

## Bernoulli race and exponential-weight selection (§3, physical pp. 6–8)

For `m` input coin/sample distributions with biases (or means) `v_i`, Algorithm 1 draws index `I` uniformly and one sample from its distribution, returning `I` on a head and otherwise repeating. Theorem 3.3 (physical p. 6) follows by a geometric-series calculation: one round terminates with probability `S/m`, where `S=Σ_i v_i`; the chance that the successful round returns i is `(v_i/m)/(S/m)=v_i/S`. Thus it samples exactly proportional to `v_i`, with expected `m/S` input samples, provided `S>0`. This is a pointwise bound: as `S↓0`, no uniform expected-cost bound follows.

The basic exponential race (Algorithm 2, Theorem 3.5, physical p. 7) first creates coins with biases `exp(λ(v_i−1))` by the Poisson generating-function subroutine, then runs the race. It outputs index i with probability `exp(λv_i)/Σ_j exp(λv_j)`. Let `S_λ=Σ_j exp(λ(v_j−1))`. The geometric race has exact expected input-sample cost `λm/S_λ`; since `S_λ≥exp(λ(v_max−1))`, this gives the source-stated upper bound `λm exp(λ(1−v_max))`. Theorem 3.5 prints that upper-bound expression as the expected cost; do not treat it as an exact equality unless all other weights vanish. The fast race (Algorithm 3, Theorem 3.7, physical pp. 7–8) uses the additive-shift invariance of exponential weights: estimate `v_max` to additive `ε=1/λ`, add a common baseline to all weights after scaling them by `1−2ε`, and run the basic race with `λ′=λ/(1−2ε)`. For `λ>4`, the source proves an expected `O(λ⁴m² log(λm))` input-sample bound. This is an expected sample-complexity guarantee for this vector-valued index sampler, not a uniform moment theorem for arbitrary scalar factories.

## Application boundaries (§§4–6, physical pp. 8–28; Appendix A, pp. 30–31)

For the single-agent multiple-urn problem, each urn’s value is a mean `v_j(t)=E_{o∼D_j}v(t,o)∈[0,1]`, accessible by samples; values are normalized and the mechanism has black-box value evaluation. Theorem 4.4 (physical p. 10) uses the exponential-weights rule, the optimizer of welfare plus entropy, to obtain an incentive-compatible additive-`ε` expected-welfare approximation with expected time `O(m²(log m/ε)^5)`. The mechanism-design conclusion uses maximal-in-range structure and the paper’s payment theorem; it is not a claim that any approximately estimated argmax is incentive compatible.

For the Bayesian black-box reduction, Sections 5.3–5.7 use independent types from known priors, replica–surrogate matching, bounded values in `[0,1]`, sample access to replica–surrogate value distributions, and an allocation oracle. Lemma 5.7 (physical p. 15) samples each surrogate according to weights `exp((v_{i,j}−α_j)/δ)` for prices `α∈[0,h]^m`; because the unnormalized utilities range over `[-h,1]`, the proof shifts by `h`, rescales by `1/(h+1)`, and adjusts the race parameter. The stated sample cost is `O(h⁴m² log(hm/δ) δ⁻⁴)` for that normalized setting. Algorithm 4 (p. 17) and Theorems 5.8–5.9 combine the per-replica maximal-in-range rule, online entropy-regularized matching and parameter choices to obtain BIC and polynomial expected runtime/welfare loss under their stated assumptions. Corollary 5.20 (p. 27) adds a doubling-dimension condition on type spaces and a market-size choice; its `3ε` welfare statement is not dimension-free when the doubling dimension is allowed to grow. Section 6’s implicit payment discussion assumes star-convex type spaces at 0. Appendix A proves stationarity of the replica-surrogate reduction and the BIC implication from a feasible truthful matching.

## Transfer limits

- The scalar definition in §2 is not the same object as an expectations-from-samples algorithm outputting an index in `[m]`.
- The linear race needs positive total mass `Σ_i v_i`; its exact law and geometric expected cost are pointwise in the input vector.
- Fast exponential-race bounds concern a specified algorithm with `λ>4`, bounded input means and the described sampling model. They do not imply uniform tails or moments for general Bernoulli factories.
- The BIC claims require the mechanism-design assumptions above; these sections are applications, not additional general scalar-factory characterization theorems.
