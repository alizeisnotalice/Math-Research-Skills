## Theorem statements and exact scope

The setting is one-dimensional Fourier multipliers on ℝ. For a symbol $m$, its $j$-th frequency component is
$$m_j(ξ)=η(ξ)m(2^jξ),$$
where $η$ is the paper's fixed even cutoff supported on $±[1/2,4]$ and equal to 1 on $±[3/4,3]$ (physical p. 2, Definition 1.1). The multiplier classes and conclusions require uniform bounds over these components.

- **Theorem 1.2 (physical p. 2; printed p. 522).** If the $m_j$ have uniformly bounded variation (the Marcinkiewicz class), then $T_m:H^1(ℝ)→L^{1,∞}(ℝ)$, and locally $L\log^rL→L^{1,q}$ whenever $r≥1/2+1/q$. The converse examples fail $H^1→L^{1,q}$ for every finite $q$, and fail the local estimate whenever $r<1/2+1/q$. The threshold is sharp within this symbol class; it is not a claim for arbitrary singular integrals.
- **Theorem 1.4 (physical p. 3; printed p. 523).** All statements of Theorem 1.2, positive and negative, continue to hold for the paper's $R_2$ class. Here the $R_2$ components lie uniformly in the atomic Banach space generated by symbols $\sum_I c_I\mathbf1_I$, with pairwise disjoint intervals $I\subset±[1/2,4]$ and $(\sum_I|c_I|^2)^{1/2}≤1$ (Definitions 1.3–1.4, physical pp. 2–3).
- **Theorem 1.6 (physical p. 4; printed p. 524).** For the smoother $R^2_{1/2,2}$ class (Definition 1.5, physical p. 3), $T_m:H^1→L^{1,2}$, and locally $L\log^rL→L^{1,q}$ if $r≥\max\{1/2,1/q\}$. Sharp converse examples fail $H^1→L^{1,q}$ for every $q<2$, and fail the local estimate below the stated $r$ threshold. This class uses $C^1_0$ interval-adapted bump atoms and the uniform localization condition $\|ψm(2^{-j}\cdot)\|_{\bar X'}\lesssim1$, with the same $ℓ^2$ coefficient convention as interval atoms in $X$. The later $R^2_{1/2,q}$ variant in Definition 1.7 adds scale-grouped $ℓ^q$ coefficients; do not conflate it with Theorem 1.6 or with plain bounded-variation multipliers.
- **Theorem 1.8 (physical p. 4).** For each $q>2$, there are $R^2_{1/2,q}$ multipliers unbounded on $L^p$ when $|1/2-1/p|>1/q$. This rules out nearby $L^1$ mapping in that class; the authors explicitly defer positive interpolation claims.

Here $L^{1,q}$ denotes Lorentz space. The paper defines “locally maps” on physical p. 1: for every compact set $K\subset\mathbb R$, extend an input $f$ supported in $K$ by zero and bound the output restricted to the same $K$,
$$\|T_m f\|_{L^{1,q}(K)}\le C_K\|f\|_{L\log^rL(K)}.$$
The constant may depend on $K$; this is not a global Orlicz-to-Lorentz mapping assertion. The proof reduction on physical p. 13 uses translation/dilation to reduce to input supported in $[0,1]$, then proves a global estimate for that localized input. These endpoint estimates are operator-class-specific. They do not establish a dimension-uniform estimate for the Euclidean Riesz transform or any unrelated kernel.

## Reusable proof chain

1. **Counterexamples, §3 (physical pp. 7–10).** The authors construct symbols and test functions to show the Lorentz/Orlicz thresholds are sharp and that $R_q$ classes can fail $L^p$ boundedness outside the stated range. The negative examples are part of the theorem boundary, not positive transfer tools.
2. **Endpoint square-function reduction, Proposition 4.1 (§4, physical pp. 10–11).** If $f$ is in the unit ball of $H^1$, or in the unit ball of $L\log^{1/2}L([-C,C])$ with mean zero, there are nonnegative $F_j$ such that $|Δ_j f(x)|\lesssim F_j*φ_j(x)$ for every $j∈ℤ,x∈ℝ$, and
$$\left\|(\sum_jF_j^2)^{1/2}\right\|_1\lesssim1.$$
The $L\log^{1/2}L$ case is delicate; the full proof is in the appendix, not merely the $H^1$ square-function characterization.
3. **Interval overlap estimate, Proposition 5.1 (§5, physical pp. 12–14; physical p. 12 rendered and visually checked).** The source assumes an interval family $\mathcal I$ with $N\ge1$ an integer and $\|\sum_{I\in\mathcal I}\mathbf1_I\|_\infty\le N$. For every $I$, the symbol $m_I$ is supported on $I$, and there is a shift $\xi_I\in I$ such that $m_I(\cdot+\xi_I)$ is a standard order-zero symbol. The order-zero symbol seminorms and the implicit constants in the pointwise domination below must be uniform over $I$ for the displayed estimate to have one common constant. Given $|f_I(x)|\lesssim F_I*\phi_I(x)$, with $\phi_I$ the kernel fixed by the source formula (6),
$$\left\|\sum_{I\in\mathcal I}T_{m_I}f_I\right\|_{L^{1,\infty}}\lesssim N^{1/2}\left\|(\sum_{I\in\mathcal I}F_I^2)^{1/2}\right\|_1.$$
If the $m_I$ are instead uniformly interval-adapted bump functions, the same right side controls $L^{1,2}$. The $N^{1/2}$ factor, support condition, shift location, uniform symbol class constants, and bump-function strengthening are part of the precise scope.
4. **Proof of the overlap estimate, §§6–7 (physical pp. 15–25).** A vector-valued Calderón–Zygmund decomposition of $F=(F_I)_I$ gives the good part an $L^2$ bound; Plancherel and overlap control its multiplier sum. The bad part has mean zero on intervals $J$, with total length controlled by $N^{1/2}\|F\|_1/α$. The proof separates frequency intervals larger than $J$ from those no larger than $J$; cancellation, kernel decay, finite overlap, and a maximal-function estimate control the two cases. The bump-symbol case uses a smooth cutoff and yields the Lorentz $L^{1,2}$ conclusion.
5. **$V^s$ discussion, §8 (physical p. 26).** Corollary 8.1 extends both positive and negative Theorem 1.2 statements to $V_s$ for $1≤s<2$, using containment in $R_2$. For $s≥2$, the endpoint question remains unresolved in the paper; the authors state a conjectural model and explain their methods do not prove it. Do not extend Corollary 8.1 to $s=2$.
6. **Appendix, §9 (physical pp. 27–37).** The full proof of Proposition 4.1 is included. It first proves a dyadic Haar version (Proposition 9.1) by induction, then averages translates to remove dyadic singularities. The function is reduced to characteristic-function data; a recursive redistribution of mass makes the $F_j$ supports overlap so their contributions sum in $ℓ^2$ rather than $ℓ^1$. The appendix is included in the full reading.

## Tool use and transfer boundary

This is an **auxiliary endpoint-multiplier source** for probability-analytic-endpoint-methods: it gives precise $H^1$, Orlicz, and Lorentz endpoint templates and a concrete interval-overlap proof. For C208 it is only an analogy/method source: C208 concerns a Euclidean Riesz-transform problem, whereas these results require one-dimensional Fourier multipliers whose dyadic components satisfy the specified $R_2$, Marcinkiewicz, or smoothed atomic hypotheses. No reduction from C208's operator to any of these classes is proved here, and no dimension dependence for C208 follows.
