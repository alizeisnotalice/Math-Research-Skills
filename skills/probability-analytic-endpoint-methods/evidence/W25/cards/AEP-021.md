## Exact martingale theorem (physical p. 4)

For \(-\infty<b<B<\infty\), \(C_{p,b,B}\) is the least constant for which every real martingale transform with predictable multipliers in \([b,B]\) obeys \(\|g\|_p\le C_{p,b,B}\|f\|_p\), for \(1<p<\infty\) (Definition 1.3, physical pp. 2–3). Theorem 1.6 is a continuous-time differential-subordination form. \(X,Y\) are real càdlàg martingales adapted to a filtration satisfying the usual conditions; with \(a=(b+B)/2\), \(r=(B-b)/2\), its hypothesis is the measure inequality

\[
d[Y-aX,Y-aX]_t\;\le\;d[rX,rX]_t\qquad(t\ge0).
\]

Then \(\sup_t\|Y_t\|_p\le C_{p,b,B}\sup_t\|X_t\|_p\), for \(1<p<\infty\), and the constant is sharp. The paper states the \(t=0\) compatibility cone \((Y_0-bX_0)(Y_0-BX_0)\le0\). This is a bracket-measure assumption; it is not merely a pointwise bound on \(Y_t/X_t\).

The discrete transform verifies the bracket condition exactly: for \(dg=v\,df\), \(v\in[b,B]\),
\[
d[g-af]^2-d[rf]^2 = (v-b)(v-B)\,d[f]^2\le0.
\]
Thus the theorem includes discrete predictable transforms. The proof of Theorem 1.6 (physical pp. 8–10) reduces discrete transforms to the bracket condition, then constructs a Bellman majorant \(U\le0\) on the initial cone \((Y-bX)(Y-BX)\le0\), concave along the allowed martingale directions after a linear change of coordinates, smooths it, and applies Itô's formula to stopped processes. The constants \(b,B\) are fixed throughout. The ordered display \(bX\le Y\le BX\) is equivalent to this product condition only when \(X\ge0\); for \(X<0\) the order reverses.

## Fourier multiplier conclusions are strong $L^p$ only (physical pp. 3–4, 6–8, 10)

Theorem 1.4 literally states a lower bound for a real, even, degree-zero homogeneous multiplier \(m\) on \(\mathbb R^d\), \(d\ge2\): if \(b,B\) are the minimum and maximum of its coordinate-axis values, then \(\|T_m\|_{L^p\to L^p}\ge C_{p,b,B}\) for \(1<p<\infty\). However, the proof in §2 (physical pp. 6–8) begins with a smooth homogeneous \(m\) in the de Leeuw step and supplies no nonsmooth approximation or pointwise-axis representative argument. The reusable proof-backed lower bound is therefore restricted to smooth homogeneous symbols (or to a broader class only after a separate continuity/Lebesgue-point interface is proved).

This representative issue is substantive. In \(d=2\), define \(m(\xi)=1\) on the line \(\{\lambda e_2:\lambda\ne0\}\) and \(m(\xi)=0\) elsewhere. This is bounded, real, even and homogeneous of degree zero, with \(m(e_1)=0\), \(m(e_2)=1\), but it vanishes almost everywhere in \(\mathbb R^2\), so its multiplier operator is zero. Meanwhile \(C_{p,0,1}\ge1\), as seen by taking the identity transform of a nonzero constant martingale. Thus arbitrary pointwise axis values cannot be used in the claimed lower bound for a general bounded representative. This example explains the scope restriction; it does not challenge the smooth-class argument or Theorem 1.5.

Theorem 1.5 is an equality for the special second-order Riesz family. For real symmetric \(A\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\le\cdots\le\lambda_d\) and \(S_A=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}R_iR_j\),
\[
\|S_A\|_{L^p(\mathbb R^d)\to L^p(\mathbb R^d)}=C_{p,\lambda_1,\lambda_d},\qquad 1<p<\infty.
\]
The lower estimate comes from Theorem 1.4 in an eigenbasis. The upper estimate uses the heat-extension stochastic-integral representation and Theorem 1.6: \(bI\le A\le BI\) implies \((A-BI)(A-bI)\preceq0\), yielding the bracket condition (proof, physical p. 10). The authors also note the same upper argument for a variable-coefficient symmetric matrix \(A(x,t)\) when \(b|\xi|^2\le\langle A(x,t)\xi,\xi\rangle\le B|\xi|^2\) everywhere (Remark 4.1, physical p. 10).

The exact theorem concerns \(1<p<\infty\). It supplies no \(p=1\) weak-type estimate, and the lower-bound multiplier theorem alone does not identify a target operator norm. The Riesz stochastic representation is a separate transfer input. No first-order vector Riesz endpoint follows from Theorem 1.5.

## Proof chain for the multiplier lower bound (physical pp. 6–8)

The proof of Theorem 1.4 first takes a smooth homogeneous multiplier to the torus by de Leeuw's theorem; it then chooses torus characters that realize the axis values \(b,B\), uses a Paley–Walsh martingale with deterministic coefficients in \(\{b,B\}\), and applies the Geiss–Montgomery-Smith–Saksman inequality. This yields \(\|T_m\|_p\ge C_{p,b,B}\) in the smooth class. Theorem 2.2 supplies the relevant torus norm comparison; its proof is not a weak-type argument. The paper does not provide the nonsmooth approximation previously attributed to it.
