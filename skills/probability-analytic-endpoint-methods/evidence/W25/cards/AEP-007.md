## Norm convention and operator scope

The paper studies **real-valued scalar inputs and one directional Riesz transform at a time**. On ℝ^d,
\[
R_jf(x)=\frac{\Gamma((d+1)/2)}{\pi^{(d+1)/2}}\,\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{x_j-y_j}{|x-y|^{d+1}}f(y)\,dy,
\]
with Fourier multiplier −iξ(_j)/|ξ|. The weak-L^p quantity used for all *sharp constants* is the rearrangement norm
\[
\|f\|_{L^{p,\infty}}=\sup_{r>0}r^{1/p}f^{**}(r),\qquad
f^*(r)=\inf\{s>0:m(|f|>s)\le r\},\quad f^{**}(r)=r^{-1}\int_0^r f^*(t)\,dt.
\]
The paper notes that this is equivalent to the more common distribution quasi-norm, but its exact constants are for the displayed (f^{**})-norm. On the sphere the same definitions use normalized rotation-invariant surface measure, with (0<r\le1).

## Main results

### Theorem 1.1: Euclidean scalar coordinates (PDF pp. 307–308)

For (f:\mathbb R^d\to[0,1]) integrable and every (r>0),
\[
r(R_jf)^{**}(r)\le \frac1\pi\int_0^r\operatorname{arsinh}\!\left(\frac{2\|f\|_1}{s}\right)ds. \tag{1.3}
\]
This is a rearrangement-integral endpoint profile for bounded nonnegative input; it is not stated as a universal weak-((1,1)) operator norm for arbitrary (L^1) input.

For (1<p<\infty),
\[
\|(R_jf)_+\|_{L^{p,\infty}(\mathbb R^d)}\le C_p^+\|f\|_{L^{p,\infty}(\mathbb R^d)},\qquad
C_p^+=\frac{2^{1-1/p}}{\pi}\int_0^\infty\frac{r^{-1/p}}{\sqrt{1+r^2}}\,dr. \tag{1.4}
\]
The constant (C_p^+) is best possible. The full absolute-value estimate is
\[
\|R_jf\|_{L^{p,\infty}(\mathbb R^d)}\le 2C_p^+\|f\|_{L^{p,\infty}(\mathbb R^d)}. \tag{1.5}
\]
The paper does not claim (1.5) is sharp. An equivalent closed form, derived from (r^2=t/(1-t)), is
\[
C_p^+=\frac{2^{-1/p}}{\pi^{3/2}}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p-1}{2p}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac1{2p}\right).
\]
The saved SymPy/mpmath calculation checks this conversion only; it does not verify the theorem or sharpness.

Theorem 1.2 gives the same (C_p^+) and (2C_p^+) inequalities for each fixed directional transform (R\in\{R^c_{\ell m},R^b_{\ell m}\}) on (S^{d-1}), (1\le\ell<m\le d). The positive-part constant is stated sharp for (d=2); the paper does not assert sharpness on higher-dimensional spheres. The sphere estimate is a one-plane directional estimate, not a sharp bound for a vector of all directional components.

### Theorem 2.2: sharp martingale profile (PDF pp. 309–311)

On a complete filtered probability space with the usual null-set condition, let (X,Y) be real adapted càdlàg martingales with left limits. They must be orthogonal (([X,Y]) is constant a.s.), (Y) differentially subordinate to (X) (([X,X]-[Y,Y]) is nonnegative and nondecreasing), (X_t\in[0,1]), and (Y_0=0). With (\Phi_\lambda(t)=(t-\lambda)_+) and (\|X\|_1=\sup_{t\ge0}\mathbb E|X_t|=\mathbb E X_0\in[0,1]),
\[
\mathbb E\Phi_\lambda(|Y_\infty|)\le \frac1\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\Phi_\lambda\!\left(\left|\frac1\pi\log|s|\right|\right)
\frac{\sin(\pi\|X\|_1)}{s^2+1+2s\cos(\pi\|X\|_1)}\,ds. \tag{2.1}
\]
The inequality is sharp for a nontrivial pair. The paper defines (\|X\|_p=\sup_{t\ge0}\|X_t\|_{L^p}). Its auxiliary Theorem 2.1 is the sharp strong-(L^p) inequality (\|Y\|_p\le\cot(\pi/(2p^*))\|X\|_p) for orthogonal (Y\prec X), (1<p<\infty), (p^*=\max(p,p/(p-1))).

The proof constructs a harmonic Poisson potential on the upper half-plane and pulls it back by a conformal map to the strip ([0,1]\times\mathbb R). Lemma 2.3 gives its exact boundary integral, concavity in the (X)-coordinate, and majorization of Φ(_\lambda(|y|)). Lemma 2.4 reduces (Y) to a continuous martingale orthogonal and subordinate to the continuous part of (X). Itô's formula then has zero-mean martingale term, zero mixed covariance term, nonpositive continuous second-order term, and nonpositive jump remainder by concavity.

**Initial-value proof correction (PDF p. 311).** In the printed proof, the source writes (U_\lambda(X_0,0)=U_\lambda(\|X\|_1,0)) “since (X) is nonnegative.” This pointwise equality is not justified when (X_0) is random, as allowed by Theorem 2.2. The needed estimate follows instead from Lemma 2.3(ii), which gives concavity in (x) on the open strip; because (U_\lambda) extends continuously to the closed strip, (x\mapsto U_\lambda(x,0)) is concave on ([0,1]), so Jensen gives
\[
\mathbb E U_\lambda(X_0,0)\le U_\lambda(\mathbb E X_0,0)
=U_\lambda(\|X\|_1,0),
\]
using (0\le X_t\le1) and the martingale identity (\|X\|_1=\sup_t\mathbb E|X_t|=\mathbb E X_0). Combining this with the nonpositive Itô drift/jump terms and Lemma 2.3(i) completes the bound. This is an explicit proof-supported Jensen correction, not endorsement of the printed equality. The saved numerical example uses the valid model (X_t=X_0) for all (t) and (Y_t\equiv0), with (X_0) equiprobable on ({1/4,3/4}); it illustrates the strict Jensen gap only. Concavity plus Jensen supplies the general argument.

The boundary majorization and Fatou yield (2.1); equality is approached by continuous orthogonal martingales with equal quadratic variations and (X_\infty\in\{0,1\}).

### Proof route for (1.3)–(1.5) and sphere analogues (PDF pp. 311–322)

For (R_j) on ℝ^d, the paper uses the Gundy–Varopoulos background-radiation representation: Brownian motion in ℝ(^{d+1}), stopped when the vertical Brownian motion reaches −y, and a skew ((d+1)\times(d+1)) matrix (A_j) representing the (j)-th coordinate. The conditional expectation (T_y^{A_j}f) tends a.e. to (R_jf). The stochastic integral martingales are orthogonal and differentially subordinate; Theorem 2.2 controls the convex excess Φ(_\lambda). Fubini and Poisson decay as (y\to\infty) give
\[
\int_{\mathbb R^d}\Phi_\lambda(|R_jf|)\,dx\le \frac{2\|f\|_1}{\pi}\int_{\pi\lambda}^\infty\frac{du}{\sinh u}.
\]
For a finite-measure set (E), split where |(R_jf)| is above/below λ; this gives an upper bound for \(\int_E|R_jf|\) by the last excess term plus λ|E|. Optimizing λ yields (1.3). For (1.4), use Fourier antisymmetry \(\int_E R_jf=-\int fR_j\chi_E\), Hardy–Littlewood rearrangement, integration by parts, and (1.3), then normalize \(\|f\|_{p,\infty}=1\). The full estimate (1.5) follows by applying the one-sided bound to positive and negative parts.

On the sphere the two operators are distinct: the cylindrical transform is (R^c_{\ell m}=T_{\ell m}(-\Delta_{S^{d-1}})^{-1/2}), and the ball transform is (R^b_{\ell m}=T_{\ell m}(\partial/\partial\nu)^{-1}), the latter acting on mean-zero spherical harmonics by multiplication by (1/k) on degree (k\ge1). In Theorem 4.1 a skew matrix field (A_{\ell m}(x)), with radial coefficient φ(|x|²), represents the ball transform for φ≡1; for (d\ge3), the stated Bessel-integral choice represents the cylindrical transform. Brownian exit from the ball has normalized uniform boundary law; conditional Jensen plus Theorem 2.2 yields the sphere analogue of (1.3), and the same rearrangement/duality argument gives Theorem 1.2.

**Printed endpoint typo (PDF p. 317).** In the §3 boundary-term estimate, the source states (\lim_{m\to\infty}II=0), then immediately sends (m\to0) and (M\to\infty). The printed upper-endpoint limit is inconsistent with that limiting procedure and is treated here as a typographical error: the lower boundary term is sent to zero as (m\downarrow0), while the separate upper endpoint is handled as (M\to\infty). The card does not use the printed (m\to\infty) limit. For the contextually intended endpoint, the displayed Theorem 1.1(i) bound gives \((R_j\chi_E)^{**}(m)\le (\pi m)^{-1}\int_0^m\operatorname{arsinh}(2|E|/s)\,ds=O(\log(1/m))\) for fixed finite \(|E|\); the weak-\(L^p\) normalization gives \(f^*(m)\le f^{**}(m)\le m^{-1/p}\). Thus both terms displayed in \(II\) are \(O(m^{1-1/p}\log(1/m))\to0\) as \(m\downarrow0\), because \(p>1\). This source-context estimate supports the correction; it is not the limit printed in the paper.

## Sharpness and limits

- In (d=1), for (E\subset\mathbb R) of finite positive measure, the paper uses \(m\{|H\chi_E|>t\}=2|E|/\sinh(\pi t)\). The indicator gives equality in (1.3); an explicit equimonotone function with \(\|f\|_{p,\infty}=1\) gives equality in (1.4).
- For (d>1), a transverse anisotropic dilation makes the Fourier multiplier of (R_1) converge in (L^2) to −i sgn(ξ(_1)); a subsequence converges a.e., and Fatou transfers any proposed smaller constant to the one-dimensional Hilbert transform. This proves sharpness of (1.3)/(1.4) for every Euclidean dimension.
- On (S^1), an exact Hilbert-circle distribution formula proves sharpness of the profile (4.2); a small-set limit proves sharpness of the positive-part constant (1.6). The article says it cannot prove optimality for (d>2) on spheres.
- There is no (p=1) weak-L^1 operator-norm theorem here. Formula (1.3) is a sharper rearrangement profile for (0\le f\le1); the (L^{p,\infty}) statements begin at (1<p<\infty).
- Do not replace the single-coordinate estimate with a vector Euclidean estimate, the sphere's one-plane operator with all components, or real input with complex input.
