## Setup and inner stationary-law theorem

Let `(S,d)` be compact metric, let `theta` be a Borel probability law on `S`, and let integers `k >= 2` and `1 <= j <= k` be fixed. From state `s`, draw `k` iid samples from `theta` and move to the `j`th closest sample, breaking ties uniformly. Write `K_{theta,j,k}` for this Markov kernel and `pi_{j,k}(theta)` for its invariant law.

**Theorem 1 (PDF p.5; proof pp.5–6).** For every such `S, theta, j, k`, the frozen kernel `K_{theta,j,k}` has a unique stationary law `pi=pi_{j,k}(theta)`. From any initial point, if `D(t)` is total-variation distance to `pi`, then for integers `t >= 1`,

`D(2t) <= (1 - k^{-(k-1)})^t`.

For every measurable `A subseteq S`,

`theta(A)^k <= pi(A) <= k theta(A)`,

so `pi` and `theta` have the same null sets and support. The rate depends on `k`, not on `S`, `theta`, or `j`.

**Proof chain.** For any measurable partition `S = disjoint_union_{i=1}^k B_i`, convexity gives `sum_i theta(B_i)^k >= k^{-(k-1)}`. Couple two chains using common fresh iid sample vectors. Conditional on the next sample vector, its nearest-rank cells partition `S`; if all samples in the preceding batch land in one cell, both chains select the same index and coalesce. This occurs with probability at least `sum_i theta(B_i)^k`, uniformly in current states. The geometric coupling tail yields total-variation Cauchy convergence of even/odd time subsequences, stationarity, and uniqueness. The set bounds follow because all `k` samples in `A` force selection into `A`, while selecting into `A` requires at least one sample in `A`.

This theorem concerns iteration of `K_{theta,j,k}` with one fixed `theta`. It does not establish convergence of the different outer map `Phi(theta)=pi_{j,k}(theta)`.

## Theorem 3: nearest/farthest invariant-law equivalence

**Theorem 3 (PDF pp.8–9).** On every compact metric space, the invariant laws of `pi_{1,2}` and `pi_{2,2}` coincide. For the nearest-of-two kernel, the density with respect to `theta` has the displayed greater-distance / tie form. The fixedpoint equation `theta K_{theta,1,2}=theta` balances integrated greater- and smaller-distance probabilities; substituting the balance into the farthest-of-two equation gives invariance there. Reversing the argument proves the converse.

## Theorem 4: interval classification for `k=2`

**Theorem 4 (PDF p.10; proof pp.10–12).** On `[0,1]`, the only invariant laws of `pi_{2,2}` are point masses `delta_s` and equal two-point laws `1/2(delta_{s_1}+delta_{s_2})`. Theorem 3 transfers the classification to `pi_{1,2}`. This result is only for `k=2`.

**Proof chain, including the printed direction issue.** By affine scaling and support-endpoint reduction, it suffices to treat a law with support endpoints 0 and 1. Lemma 5 rules out a zero atom at a support endpoint. The source's printed proof of Lemma 5 (PDF p.10) defines `X` as the farther of iid `Y_1,Y_2` from `Y`; on the cross event `Y_1 <= x < Y_2`, its event `X <= x` requires `Y >= (Y_1+Y_2)/2`. The next claimed inclusion uses `Y <= (x+Y_2)/2`, which does **not** follow from that condition. The exact rational check `x=1/5, Y_1=1/10, Y_2=9/10, Y=4/5` satisfies the printed event and violates the claimed upper condition; see `evidence/shared/reviews/R02/batch5-S025-direction-check.json`. This is an internal proof-direction typo, not a counterexample to the theorem statement.

A valid repair uses the paper's Theorem 3 before Lemma 5: transfer the invariant law from the farthest-of-two kernel to the nearest-of-two kernel, and redefine `X` as the nearer of `Y_1,Y_2`. On `Y_1 <= x < Y_2`, the event `X <= x` requires `Y <= (Y_1+Y_2)/2`; since `Y_1 <= x`, this implies `Y <= (x+Y_2)/2`. The symmetric cross event gives the other term, so independence yields the displayed source inequality

`F(x) <= F(x)^2 + 2 F(x) P(Y <= (x+Y_2)/2)`.

For `F(x)>0`, divide by `F(x)` and let `x downarrow 0` to get `P(Y <= Y_2/2) >= 1/2`. Symmetry gives `P(Y_2 <= Y/2) >= 1/2`; since there is no endpoint atom at 0, these disjoint events force `P(Y/2 < Y_2 < 2Y)=0`. Conditioning iid samples on a common interval `[y,3y/2] subset (0,1]` contradicts this. Thus the endpoint atom must be positive. Lemma 6 then uses stationarity at 0 and 1 plus `x >= x^2 + 2x(1-x) beta => beta <= 1/2` to force equal endpoint masses and support `{0,1}`. Reflection and the support-endpoint reduction complete the classification. The nearest-kernel replacement is the explicit proof repair; it is not the direction printed in the original farthest-kernel argument.

## Theorem 7: one finite tree-space nonexistence class

**Theorem 7 (PDF pp.12–14).** For a finite binary-tree-leaf metric with at least three leaves and distinct distances from each leaf, the `k=2` map has no full-support invariant law. The proof divides into a leaf of mass greater than `1/2`, excluded by a sufficient event bound, and the case where all leaf masses are at most `1/2`; a centroid split then produces a strict lower bound on the nearest/farthest output probability and an impossible three-cell mass inequality. This is a special finite metric class, not a statement for arbitrary compact spaces.

## Application boundary

- For A121, Theorem 1 supplies a unique frozen-`theta` stationary law and inner total-variation mixing. It supplies no outer contraction, no-cycle theorem, basin estimate, or almost-everywhere convergence for `theta[n+1]=pi_{j,k}(theta[n])`.
- For A122, Theorem 4 handles `k=2` only. It does not classify general `(j,k)` full-support Borel laws.
- The no-distance-ties continuity result often associated with this paper is **not** here; it is Proposition 2 in W21-002, stated on that manuscript's physical p.7 with proof on pp.8–9.
