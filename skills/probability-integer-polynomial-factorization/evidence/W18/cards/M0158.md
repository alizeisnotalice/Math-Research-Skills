## Model and exact scope

`H_{n,d}` denotes real homogeneous forms of degree `d` in `n` variables. `P_{n,2d}` is the cone of forms nonnegative **at every point of** `R^n`; `Σ_{n,2d}` is the cone of sums of squares of degree-`d` forms. The article's main cases are ternary sextics `(n,d)=(3,3)` and quaternary quartics `(n,d)=(4,2)`: `P_{3,6}` versus `Σ_{3,6}`, and `P_{4,4}` versus `Σ_{4,4}`. Positivity is pointwise on all real inputs, not coefficientwise positivity.

## Main statements and proof chain

- **Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 (physical p. 2).** If `p∈P_{3,6}\Σ_{3,6}`, there are two real cubics whose common projective zero set has nine points `γ_i` (possibly complex), and a real linear functional `ℓ:H_{3,6}→R` represented by weighted evaluations `ℓ(f)=Σ_i μ_i f(z_i)` at affine representatives, with `ℓ(r)≥0` for every `r∈Σ_{3,6}` but `ℓ(p)<0`; at most two of the nine points are nonreal. The `(4,4)` statement is parallel: three real quadrics in `H_{4,2}`, eight intersection points, a separator nonnegative on `Σ_{4,4}` and negative on `p`, at most two nonreal points. The evaluation data for complex points occur in conjugate pairs so the functional is real. The proof later selects a transverse intersection.
- **Corollaries 1.3 and 1.4 (physical p. 3).** A strictly positive SOS form on the boundary of `Σ_{3,6}` is a sum of three squares but not two; in the `(4,4)` case, it is a sum of four squares but not three. These are square-count statements for boundary points of the stated SOS cones.
- **Theorems 1.5 / 3.4 and Corollary 3.5 (physical pp. 3, 10–11).** For a completely real transverse intersection of two ternary cubics (nine points) or three quaternary quadrics (eight points), take affine representatives so that the unique Cayley–Bacharach relation on degree-`d` forms has coefficients `a_i=±1`. Under evaluation at these points, the image `Sq` of the SOS cone is exactly
  `T_m={x∈R_+^m : Σ_i sqrt(x_i) ≥ 2 sqrt(x_k) for every k}`.
  With arbitrary affine representatives and nonzero real relation coefficients `u_i`, Corollary 3.5 gives the equivalent weighted inequalities `Σ_i |u_i|sqrt(x_i) ≥ 2|u_k|sqrt(x_k)` for every `k`. The nonnegative-form image contains the strictly positive orthant (Corollary 3.2); that is not an assertion that the SOS and psd images coincide. Mechanism: a degree-`d` evaluation vector `s_i=q(v_i)` satisfies the Cayley–Bacharach relation, so `x_i=s_i²` obeys the triangle inequality defining `T_m`. Conversely, Lemma 3.3 shows `T_m` is the convex hull of its equality-boundary points; for each such point choose signed square roots satisfying the relation, realize them as evaluations of a degree-`d` form, and square it.
- **Dual SOS cone structure (Theorems 1.6/1.7, 2.7; physical pp. 4, 7).** Associate to a linear functional `ℓ` on `H_{n,2d}` the quadratic form `Q_ℓ(f)=ℓ(f²)` on `H_{n,d}`. In the two stated cases, every extreme ray of the dual SOS cone has rank `1` (point evaluation) or rank `dim(H_{n,d})−n`; these are ranks `1 or 7` for `(3,6)` and `1 or 6` for `(4,4)`. Lemma 2.1 (physical p. 4) states the dual-cone identity `Σ*_{n,2d}=S^+_{n,d}∩H*_{n,2d}`; both stars are essential. The proof then uses the maximal-kernel criterion for extreme rays of a section of the PSD cone (Lemma 2.2), a complete-intersection parameter sequence with Gorenstein socle degree `n(d−1)=2d` (Theorem 2.5), and the kernel identity `p∈ker Q_ℓ` iff `ℓ(pq)=0` for all `q∈H_{n,d}` (Lemma 2.6). Rank-one rays are exactly point evaluations (Lemma 2.4); any other extreme-ray kernel has no common real or complex projective zero (Corollary 2.3).
- **Cayley–Bacharach and main separation proof (Lemmas 2.8–2.9, 4.1–4.4; physical pp. 7–13).** Bertini's theorem gives `n−1` real members of the `n`-dimensional kernel intersecting transversely; their intersection has `d^{n−1}` projective points (9 or 8). The Cayley–Bacharach theorem gives a unique linear relation among the values of degree-`d` forms at these points. Lemma 4.1 expresses the separator weights as `μ_i=u_i/f_n(z_i)`. Lemma 4.2 shows that if `Q_ℓ` is PSD, the real evaluation image dimension is at most the number of real points plus the number of conjugate pairs; Corollaries 4.3–4.4 then bound complex pairs by the independent evaluation relations, yielding at most one pair in these cases. Section 5 combines this with separation of the psd and SOS cones to prove Theorems 1.1–1.2.
- **Fully real and one-complex-pair extreme rays (Theorems 6.1 and 7.1; physical pp. 14–16).** For a fully real transverse intersection with unique relation coefficients `u_i`, Theorem 6.1 represents an extreme dual-ray quadratic form as `Q_ℓ(f)=Σ_i a_i f(v_i)²`, with exactly one negative `a_k`, all others positive, and `Σ_i u_i²/a_i=0`; any such form is extreme. Proposition 6.2 derives the boundary condition by restricting a diagonal quadratic form to the Cayley–Bacharach hyperplane and using a Lagrange-multiplier/Schur-complement calculation. For one conjugate pair `z,\bar z`, **Theorem 7.1's printed display on physical p. 15 is internally inconsistent with its proof**: it writes `4m(Re z)²−4t(Im z)²` (without `f` and without a mixed term), while the proof on p. 16 starts from `b=m+it` and the evaluation coordinates `x=2 Re f(z)`, `y=2 Im f(z)`. Those proof steps give the complex-pair contribution `2m((Re f(z))²−(Im f(z))²)−4t Re f(z) Im f(z)=(m/2)(x²−y²)−txy`. Completing the square gives `[(m²+t²)/(2m)]x²−(tx+my)²/(2m)`, with `m<0`. The p. 16 intermediate `Q'` line prints `(m²+t²)/m` instead of `(m²+t²)/(2m)`; the following relation and the theorem's final constraint are consistent with the latter coefficient and give `2m/(m²+t²)+Σ_i u_i²/a_i=0`, with all `a_i>0`. The card records this proof-supported calculation and flags the apparent original typographical inconsistencies; it does not infer that the whole theorem is false. Any use of the statement should preserve this caveat.
- **Conjecture 7.3 (physical p. 16).** In the `(3,6)` and `(4,4)` cases, if every transverse intersection formed from `n−1` members of a given `n`-dimensional space `W` with `V_C(W)=∅` has at most one complex pair, then there is such an intersection with all points real. This is explicitly a conjecture, not a proved step or theorem.

## A175 interface and non-transfer

A175 asks about ordinary univariate probability-generating polynomials with finite supports (possibly gaps), whose coefficients are nonnegative reals and whose product has constant 0–1 coefficients. This paper studies global pointwise nonnegativity of homogeneous multivariate forms versus SOS representations, and only gives detailed cone theorems in the fixed `(3,6)` and `(4,4)` settings. A nonnegative coefficient list is not the same condition as a polynomial being nonnegative on all of `R`: for example, `1+x` has nonnegative coefficients but is negative at `x=−2`. Thus its factors do not automatically belong to `P_{n,2d}`; Cayley–Bacharach relations among projective evaluation points are also not coefficient-convolution relations. Even if an A175 polynomial were independently shown to be globally nonnegative as a univariate polynomial, the univariate psd/SOS theorem would not force its coefficients to be uniform or establish the A175 factorization conclusion.

**Tool use:** advisory/cautionary auxiliary only. A possible SOS certificate can be considered only after a separate argument establishes global nonnegativity and an appropriate homogeneous-form setting. No source theorem here supplies that bridge, handles coefficientwise-nonnegative factors, or proves/refutes A175.
