**Identity and reading evidence.** Theodore S. Motzkin and Ernst G. All 15 physical pages were read; physical pp.2,3,6–13 were rendered and viewed for the theorem statements, formulae, and proof transitions. ## Terminology that controls the statements

On printed p.641 (PDF physical p.2), “positive” for a polynomial means **coefficientwise nonnegative and not identically zero**. “Copositive” means its value is strictly positive whenever all variables are nonnegative and not all are zero. These are different notions: copositivity does not imply nonnegative coefficients. For example, (1+(x-y)^2) is copositive on the nonnegative orthant but has a negative (xy)-coefficient. The article’s hypotheses on the lowest and highest homogeneous pieces are substantive; merely having no zero in the finite orthant is not enough for all conclusions.

The introduction states two directions. A real divisor (Q) of a coefficientwise-positive polynomial (P) with (P(0)\ne0) is copositive (after changing its sign if needed). Conversely, a real copositive (Q) whose highest and lowest homogeneous pieces are copositive has positive polynomial multiples of the types listed in the article. None of this says that the factors have integer or 0–1 coefficients.

## Theorem 2.1 — minimum degree and extremal multiples (printed pp.642–643; PDF pp.3–4)

Let \(0<\theta<\pi\), \(r>0\), and let \(n\) be the least integer with \((n-1)\theta<\pi\le n\theta\). For the quadratic with roots \(re^{\pm i\theta}\),
\[
Q_r(x)=x^2-2r\cos\theta\,x+r^2,
\]
the minimum degree of a nonzero coefficientwise-nonnegative multiple is at least \(n\). If \(n\theta=\pi\), the unique monic degree-\(n\) positive multiple is \(x^n+r^n\). If \(n\theta>\pi\), for each \(m=1,\ldots,n-1\), the source gives the positive monic trinomial
\[
P_m(x)=x^n-\frac{\sin(n\theta)}{\sin(m\theta)}r^{n-m}x^m
+\frac{\sin((n-m)\theta)}{\sin(m\theta)}r^n.
\]
It states these are the vertices of the simplex of monic degree-\(n\) positive multiples of \(Q_r\); any such multiple divided by \(Q_r\) has all coefficients strictly positive.

**Printed discrepancy (not silently corrected).** Physical p.2 prints the correct \(Q_r=x^2-2r\cos\theta\,x+r^2\), and the proof on p.3 uses roots \(re^{\pm i\theta}\). But the theorem statement on physical p.3 drops the factor \(r\) in the middle coefficient, printing \(Q=x^2-2\cos\theta\,x+r^2\); the displayed trinomial and its proof use powers of \(r\) consistent with \(Q_r\). The exact division check saved with this batch verifies the root-consistent formula. The card separates that derived correction from the literal theorem line.

**Proof mechanism.** If a positive polynomial has degree (d<n), evaluate it at (re^{i\theta}). Its nonnegative coefficient terms point in directions (0,\theta,\ldots,d\theta), all lying in an open half-plane because (d\theta<\pi); their nontrivial positive linear combination cannot vanish. At degree (n), the boundary case (n\theta=\pi) gives the unique cancellation (1+r^n e^{in\theta}=0). In the strict case, the vectors (1), (r^m e^{im\theta}), and (r^n e^{in\theta}) admit the unique positive linear relation yielding the displayed trinomial. Polynomial division supplies a quotient with strictly positive coefficients. The paper then identifies the full simplex by subtracting extremal multiples until a coefficient vanishes; a residual lower-degree positive multiple would contradict the degree bound.

Corollaries 2.3–2.4 (printed pp.643–644; PDF pp.4–5) give positive **integral-coefficient** multipliers in the one-variable setting and a degree estimate under the stated root-argument restriction. Theorem 2.5 gives a sparsity alternative for minimum-degree positive multiples: either at least (n-d+1) distinct ((d+1))-nomials or at least one (d)-nomial, for a monic degree-(d) real (Q) with no nonnegative real zero. Theorem 2.6 (printed p.645; PDF p.6) states that if (\cos\theta\le m/(m+2)), then
\[
\bigl(x^2-2r\cos\theta\,x+r^2\bigr)(x+r)^m
\]
is positive; its derivative-at-zero computation gives nonnegative coefficients, and the proof notes that choosing (m) odd makes all the displayed derivatives strictly positive. Corollary 2.7 then says any real polynomial without nonnegative real zeros can be multiplied by a polynomial having only negative real zeros so the product is positive; it further permits all product coefficients positive and the multiplier coefficients integral. These are existence results for positive multipliers, not classifications of all factors.

## Several variables (printed pp.646–648; PDF pp.7–9)

For a real polynomial (Q(x)) with no zero on the nonnegative orthant, the paper additionally assumes that its leading homogeneous part has no zero on the nonnegative unit sphere (“no zero at infinity”). Theorem 3.2 then constructs positive univariate multipliers (R_i(x_i)) so that every homogeneous part of (R_1\cdots R_n Q) is positive on the orthant unit sphere. Its proof applies the one-variable result along each radial ray (x=r\xi), takes a neighborhood where the radial multiplier still works, extracts a finite cover by Heine–Borel, and combines the local multipliers coordinatewise.

Lemma 3.3 handles a homogeneous (Q) that is strictly positive on the orthant unit sphere: it constructs a homogeneous positive-coefficient multiplier by induction on dimension. Theorem 3.4 applies when (Q) is positive on the nonnegative orthant and its leading homogeneous part is positive on the nonnegative orthant unit sphere; it gives positive univariate multipliers (R_i(x_i)) and positive homogeneous multipliers (S_j(x)) so their product with (Q) has nonnegative coefficients. If (Q(0)=0) is allowed, it requires the analogous positivity assumption on the trailing (lowest-degree) homogeneous part. Theorem 3.6 states a more explicit coefficient conclusion: if (Q) is nonzero on the orthant and its leading homogeneous part is nonzero on the orthant unit sphere, a positive (R) can be chosen so that (RQ) has positive coefficients at every monomial degree no greater than (\deg(RQ)); if (Q(0)=0), the source only asserts positivity for monomials no lower than the degree of the trailing part of (RQ). Its proof perturbs coefficients degree-by-degree using binomial multipliers, bounded coefficient ratios, compactness of the orthant sphere, and a finite set of neighborhoods. The source remarks that Theorem 3.6's coefficient conclusions differ from Theorem 3.4's homogeneous-piece conclusion.

## Power series (printed pp.648–652; PDF pp.9–13)

Theorem 4.1 treats a real power series (f) of radius (r>0), normalized by (f(0)=1), with no zero on ([0,r)). It constructs a positive-coefficient power series (P) of radius at least (r) for which (P/f) is analytic on (|z|<r). The proof pairs the nonreal zeros of (f) and uses factors with exponents (N_j) chosen so that (N_j\arg z_j) lies in the closed second quadrant, with radii chosen to control convergence; the infinite product has nonnegative coefficients and cancels the zeros to the required multiplicities. Problem 4.2 asks a strengthening and is left open.

Lemma 4.3 uses the normalized logarithm (log f=\sum b_kz^k) for a zero-free (f) on the disk and (g=\exp(\sum |b_k|z^k)), yielding a positive-coefficient product (fg) on the disk. Corollary 4.4 combines this with polynomial factors. Theorem 4.5 only guarantees the multiplier-series radius for every smaller ρ<r; it does not claim the endpoint radius (r) for that strengthened conclusion. Theorem 4.6 is multivariate and local-in-coordinate: if (f) is positive on a box and analytic in a polydisc, then for each vector of smaller radii ρν<rν it supplies separate one-variable positive multipliers such that coefficients in one variable are positive functions of the other coordinates on the corresponding smaller rectangle. Remark 4.7 says this does **not** guarantee one multivariate multiplier with globally positive coefficients. The proof uses uniform convergence/Cauchy estimates, equicontinuity, and a finite Heine–Borel cover.

## A175 interface and non-transfer

Conditional method interface: for a real univariate polynomial \(Q\) with no nonnegative real zeros, Corollary 2.7 supplies a polynomial multiplier \(R\) with only negative real zeros such that \(RQ\) has nonnegative coefficients; the source also says one may require every coefficient of \(RQ\) to be strictly positive and the coefficients of \(R\) to be integers. For a quadratic whose roots are \(re^{\pm i\theta}\), Theorem 2.1 gives the degree threshold determined by \((n-1)\theta<\pi\le n\theta\) and the extremal degree-\(n\) multiples, subject to the missing-\(r\) print error isolated above. In several variables, Theorems 3.2/3.4/3.6 require orthant nonvanishing plus the stated leading homogeneous condition, and for a zero constant term the analogous trailing homogeneous condition. These are reusable conditional polynomial-multiplier/root-angle methods, so this source is a tool-source candidate even though its input class is real-coefficient polynomials rather than integer/0–1 factors. They do not establish that A175 factors are integer or Boolean, or solve the uniform-convolution question.
